Applied pelvic anatomy: obstetrics, surgery, imaging and clinical localisation

Connect pelvic anatomy to childbirth, pelvic trauma, ureteric injury, prolapse, prostate and bladder relations, and cross-sectional imaging.

Return to pelvis & perineum courseBrowse all topics

1. Obstetric anatomy and the birth canal

The bony birth canal is described through the pelvic inlet, cavity and outlet. The fetal head engages and descends through a curved canal, while pelvic joints, soft tissues, pelvic-floor muscles and fetal position influence the mechanics of labour. The obstetric conjugate is the shortest anteroposterior diameter of the inlet between the sacral promontory and posterior surface of the pubic symphysis; it is not directly measured by routine digital examination. Clinical pelvimetry and intrapartum decisions require trained obstetric assessment and should not be reduced to a single textbook diameter.

The pelvic floor stretches during vaginal birth. The perineal body and anal sphincter complex are important soft-tissue structures; tears vary in extent and can affect continence or support. The levator ani may be stretched or injured. Risk and outcome depend on multiple maternal, fetal and clinical factors. This anatomy explains why careful assessment and follow-up after significant perineal trauma matter.

2. Ureter and pelvic surgery

The ureter is vulnerable where it approaches the reproductive organs and bladder. In females, the ureter passes beneath the uterine artery near the cervix; it also lies close to the lateral vaginal fornix and uterosacral region. In males, the ductus deferens crosses over the ureter near the bladder. These relations are important in pelvic surgery, but exact course varies and must be identified directly during procedures. Ureteric obstruction or injury may cause pain, urinary leakage, infection or impaired renal function and requires clinical investigation.

3. Pelvic trauma and haemorrhage

The pelvis contains major arterial branches and extensive venous plexuses. High-energy pelvic-ring injury can produce life-threatening haemorrhage and damage the bladder, urethra, rectum, reproductive organs or lumbosacral nerves. Blood at the urethral meatus, inability to urinate, perineal bruising, pelvic instability or shock in the setting of trauma are concerning signs, but absence of one sign does not exclude injury. Emergency trauma assessment takes priority over detailed bedside manipulation.

4. Prostate, bladder and outlet obstruction

The prostate lies inferior to the bladder and surrounds the prostatic urethra. Its transition zone commonly contributes to benign prostatic enlargement, which can narrow the urethral outlet; prostate cancer often arises in the peripheral zone, although disease is heterogeneous. Digital rectal examination assesses only the accessible posterior surface and cannot independently rule in or rule out disease. Urinary symptoms may result from prostate, bladder, urethral, neurological, medication-related or other causes and require appropriate assessment.

5. Imaging anatomy

On CT, the bony pelvis provides a stable landmark framework, while bladder filling, bowel contents and contrast phase affect the appearance of soft tissues and vessels. MRI provides superior soft-tissue contrast for many pelvic structures, including uterus, prostate, rectum, pelvic floor and tumour staging. Ultrasound is often used for bladder volume and selected reproductive or pelvic assessments. The peritoneal reflections, pelvic sidewall, sacrum, obturator internus, piriformis and iliac vessels are useful orientation landmarks. Students should correlate axial images with coronal and sagittal views and confirm orientation before naming a structure.

6. Clinical localisation checklist

  • Identify the patient’s orientation and imaging plane before locating organs.
  • Use bone, vessels and muscles as landmarks; do not identify a small soft-tissue structure in isolation.
  • Relate urinary symptoms to bladder, ureter, urethra, pelvic floor and neural control rather than assuming one cause.
  • Relate perineal sensory symptoms to pudendal branches and sacral roots, but consider other neurological and local causes.
  • For pelvic pain, bleeding, trauma, pregnancy-related symptoms or urinary retention, use appropriate clinical pathways rather than self-diagnosis.

Red flags

Major trauma with faintness or shock, severe pelvic or abdominal pain, heavy bleeding, inability to pass urine, fever with severe pelvic pain, or saddle numbness with new bladder/bowel dysfunction needs urgent medical assessment.

Self-test

  1. Why is the ureter important in pelvic surgery?
    Answer

    It passes close to reproductive structures and can be injured unless its course is identified.

  2. Why can pelvic fractures be life-threatening?
    Answer

    They may disrupt arterial branches or venous plexuses and injure pelvic organs.

  3. Why should pelvic imaging be reviewed in multiple planes?
    Answer

    Cross-sectional structures can look similar in one plane; multiplanar correlation improves orientation and identification.

Study and safety note

Applied pelvic anatomy: interpreting clinical scenarios

Obstetric mechanics and soft-tissue injury

The bony birth canal is considered at the inlet, midpelvis and outlet, but labour is a dynamic interaction among fetal lie, presentation, position, head flexion and moulding, uterine activity, maternal posture, pelvic-joint mobility and soft-tissue resistance. The ischial spines are an important landmark for assessing station during labour. The obstetric conjugate is the shortest anteroposterior inlet diameter and is estimated indirectly; a single pelvic measurement cannot predict labour outcome. Clinical decisions require the whole obstetric picture and trained assessment.

The pelvic floor and perineum stretch during vaginal birth. The perineal body, external anal sphincter and levator ani are clinically important because injury can affect support, continence and pelvic function. Perineal tears vary in depth and structures involved; accurate classification, appropriate repair and follow-up are essential. Persistent symptoms after childbirth deserve assessment rather than being dismissed as inevitable.

Ureteric relations in pelvic surgery

The ureter descends retroperitoneally and enters the pelvis near the bifurcation of the common iliac artery or over the iliac vessels, with individual variation. It then runs along the lateral pelvic wall before turning medially toward the bladder. In females, the ureter passes inferior to the uterine artery near the cervix—often remembered as “water under the bridge,” where urine is the water and the uterine artery is the bridge. It is also close to the cervix, lateral vaginal fornix and uterosacral region. In males, the ductus deferens crosses superior to the ureter near the bladder. These relationships matter in hysterectomy, pelvic lymph-node dissection, colorectal surgery and procedures near the bladder base.

Pelvic trauma and haemorrhage

A pelvic-ring fracture may involve more than one fracture site and can accompany injury to the bladder, urethra, rectum, pelvic vessels or lumbosacral nerves. The pelvic venous plexuses and cancellous bone can contribute to major haemorrhage. Blood at the urethral meatus, inability to void, perineal bruising, gross haematuria, shock or neurological deficits after trauma require urgent emergency assessment. Do not insert a urethral catheter when urethral injury is suspected until appropriate clinical evaluation has occurred.

Imaging-based localisation

On axial CT, orient anteriorly with the pubic symphysis and posteriorly with the sacrum; identify bladder, rectum, obturator internus and pelvic sidewall before tracing smaller structures. CT is useful for acute fractures and many abdominal-pelvic emergencies; MRI better distinguishes many soft tissues, pelvic-floor muscles and marrow abnormalities; ultrasound is useful for selected bladder, reproductive and obstetric questions. Contrast phases, patient position and organ filling can alter appearances. Follow structures across contiguous slices rather than naming them from a single image.

Clinical reasoning examples

  • Groin or pelvic pain with sensory change: map sensory territory and examine motor function before attributing symptoms to one nerve.
  • New urinary retention with saddle numbness: consider urgent cauda equina or sacral nerve pathology.
  • Postoperative flank pain, fever or urine leakage: ureteric injury is one possibility requiring prompt specialist assessment.
  • Pelvic fracture with haemodynamic instability: treat as a potential life-threatening emergency; anatomical knowledge supports but does not replace trauma protocols.
