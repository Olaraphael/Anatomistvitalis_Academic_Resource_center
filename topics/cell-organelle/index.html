Cell structure, organelles and tissue organisation

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of the human body. A typical human cell contains a plasma membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus, but specialised cells vary substantially in shape, organelles and function. Cells interact with extracellular matrix and with neighbouring cells to form tissues; tissues combine into organs, and organs cooperate within organ systems. The nucleus contains most cellular DNA and supports transcription. Mitochondria generate much of the ATP used by aerobic cells and participate in other processes including apoptosis. Rough endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes support synthesis of many secreted and membrane proteins; smooth endoplasmic reticulum participates in lipid synthesis, calcium handling and detoxification. The Golgi apparatus modifies, sorts and packages proteins and lipids. Lysosomes contain enzymes for intracellular digestion, while peroxisomes carry out selected oxidative reactions. The cytoskeleton provides shape, intracellular transport and mechanical support. Not every cell contains every organelle in the same abundance. Mature red blood cells, for example, lack a nucleus and mitochondria; secretory cells have prominent protein-processing machinery.

Learning objectives

  • Identify major cellular structures and their functions.
  • Explain how cells, tissues, organs and systems are related.
  • Recognise that cell structure varies with specialised function.

High-yield study points

  • The plasma membrane provides selective exchange and cell signalling.
  • The nucleus stores most DNA and regulates gene expression.
  • Mitochondria produce ATP through aerobic metabolism and have additional signalling roles.
  • Rough ER and Golgi support processing and delivery of many secreted proteins.
  • Lysosomes digest cellular material; the cytoskeleton supports shape and transport.
  • Tissue is an organised group of cells and extracellular components working together.

Clinical connection

Cell injury can affect organelles differently. Mitochondrial dysfunction may particularly affect energy-demanding tissues, while defects in lysosomal enzymes can cause intracellular storage disorders.
