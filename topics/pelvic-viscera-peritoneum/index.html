Pelvic viscera, fascial planes and peritoneal relationships

Learn the spatial relationships of the urinary bladder, ureters, rectum and reproductive organs, including pelvic peritoneum and clinically relevant spaces.

1. General organisation of the pelvic cavity

The pelvic cavity is the inferior continuation of the abdominopelvic cavity. Its contents include the urinary bladder and distal ureters, rectum, internal reproductive organs, vessels, lymphatics, nerves, connective tissue and peritoneal reflections. The exact organ arrangement differs with sex, age, pregnancy, bladder filling, rectal contents and previous surgery. Pelvic organs are supported not only by muscle but also by fascia, ligaments, connective tissue condensations and the surrounding bony framework.

Peritoneum

The peritoneum descends from the abdominal cavity and reflects over selected pelvic organs, forming pouches and recesses. In the male pelvis it reflects between bladder and rectum to form the rectovesical pouch. In the female pelvis, reflections create the vesicouterine pouch between bladder and uterus and the rectouterine pouch (pouch of Douglas) between uterus and rectum. The deepest dependent space changes with posture and individual anatomy. Not every pelvic organ is completely covered by peritoneum; portions are subperitoneal or extraperitoneal.

2. Urinary bladder and distal ureters

The bladder is a distensible muscular reservoir. Its apex is directed anteriorly and may connect toward the umbilicus through the median umbilical ligament, the remnant of the urachus. The base/fundus lies posteriorly; the neck is the relatively fixed inferior region continuous with the urethra. The trigone is a smooth triangular area between the ureteric openings and internal urethral orifice. The detrusor muscle contracts during voiding, while outlet resistance depends on smooth and striated sphincter mechanisms, pelvic-floor function and neural coordination.

The ureters enter the bladder obliquely through its wall, a course that helps resist reflux during bladder filling and contraction. In the pelvis, the ureter is closely related to reproductive structures. In females, the uterine artery crosses superior to the ureter near the cervix (“water under the bridge”: ureter beneath uterine artery); this relation matters in pelvic surgery. In males, the ureter passes deep to the ductus deferens near the bladder. Relations vary, and surgical identification is essential.

3. Rectum and anal canal

The rectum follows the sigmoid colon and lies anterior to the sacrum and coccyx. Its curves and lateral flexures accommodate pelvic geometry. The rectum stores faeces before defaecation; rectal distension triggers reflex pathways that interact with voluntary control. The anal canal passes through the pelvic floor and perineum. The internal anal sphincter is smooth muscle under autonomic/intrinsic control, while the external anal sphincter is striated muscle with somatic control, principally via inferior rectal branches of the pudendal nerve and contributions from related sacral pathways.

In females, the rectum is posterior to the vagina and uterus; in males, it is posterior to the bladder and prostate. These relations explain why pelvic examination and imaging may assess adjacent structures, but clinical examination must be performed by trained professionals with appropriate consent.

4. Reproductive organs and support

In the female pelvis, the uterus is usually positioned between bladder and rectum, with the cervix projecting into the upper vagina. The ovaries lie on the lateral pelvic walls, with positions that can vary; uterine tubes extend toward them. The broad ligament is a double layer of peritoneum that acts as a mesentery-like fold but is not the sole or primary mechanical support of the uterus. Support also involves the pelvic floor, endopelvic fascia and ligamentous condensations around the cervix and vagina.

In the male pelvis, the prostate lies inferior to the bladder and surrounds the proximal urethra; seminal vesicles lie posterior to the bladder and anterior to the rectum. The ductus deferens crosses the ureter near the bladder before joining the duct of the seminal vesicle to form the ejaculatory duct. The pelvic urethra, prostate and bladder neck are functionally related to urinary continence and ejaculation.

5. Fascial spaces and clinical relevance

Endopelvic fascia surrounds and supports pelvic viscera and conveys vessels and nerves. Loose connective-tissue planes can permit spread of blood, infection, tumour or urine; the pattern depends on the site of injury and fascial attachments. The retropubic (prevesical) space lies anterior to the bladder and posterior to the pubic symphysis. Rectouterine and vesicouterine pouches are peritoneal spaces, whereas many surgical planes around the bladder, cervix, prostate and rectum are extraperitoneal. Do not use one schematic to assume every patient’s exact tissue planes.

Clinical correlation: pelvic pain, urinary retention, rectal bleeding, abnormal vaginal bleeding or a pelvic mass require appropriate medical assessment. Sudden severe pain, heavy bleeding, fainting or inability to pass urine can be urgent.

Self-test

  1. Which pouch is the most dependent peritoneal recess in the female pelvis when upright?
    Answer

    The rectouterine pouch, though position and individual anatomy affect fluid distribution.

  2. What is the “water under the bridge” relation?
    Answer

    The ureter passes beneath the uterine artery near the cervix.

  3. Why is the broad ligament not the only uterine support?
    Answer

    Pelvic-floor muscles, endopelvic fascia and ligamentous condensations provide major support.

Study and safety note

This is a learning resource for anatomy study, not a substitute for supervised dissection, clinical training or professional medical judgement. Anatomical patterns vary; clinical symptoms must be interpreted by qualified professionals.
