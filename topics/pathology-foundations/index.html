CLINICAL SCIENCES • GENERAL PATHOLOGY

Pathology Foundations: How Disease Develops

Pathology studies the causes, mechanisms, structural changes and consequences of disease. It links normal anatomy and physiology to the changes observed in illness.

Written by Ibine Oladipupo R. · Anatomistvitalis Academic Resource Center

Learning objectives: Explain the scope of pathology; distinguish aetiology from pathogenesis; describe cellular adaptation and injury; outline inflammation, repair, haemodynamic disorders and neoplasia; and connect pathological changes with clinical findings.

1. Meaning and branches of pathology

Pathology is the scientific study of disease. It examines why disease occurs (aetiology), how it develops (pathogenesis), the structural and molecular changes it produces, and the functional consequences for the organism. General pathology studies mechanisms shared by many diseases; systemic pathology applies those mechanisms to particular organs. Anatomical pathology evaluates tissues, organs and cells, while clinical pathology or laboratory medicine analyses samples such as blood, urine and other body fluids. Molecular pathology investigates disease-related changes in genes, proteins and other molecular components.

Pathological evidence may come from gross examination, histology, cytology, microbiology, immunohistochemistry, molecular testing, imaging and clinical laboratory measurements. No single test should be interpreted in isolation: findings must be considered alongside the clinical history, examination, specimen quality and test limitations.

2. Aetiology, pathogenesis, morphology and clinical consequences

Four connected concepts organise the study of disease. Aetiology is the cause or contributing factors, which may be genetic, infectious, immune-mediated, nutritional, toxic, vascular, traumatic, degenerative or multifactorial. Pathogenesis is the sequence of mechanisms through which the cause produces disease. Morphology describes structural changes at gross, microscopic or molecular levels. Clinical manifestations are the symptoms, signs, laboratory abnormalities and complications experienced or detected.

For example, reduced blood flow can deprive tissue of oxygen and substrates. If severe or prolonged, cellular energy failure and membrane damage may produce cell death; tissue function falls, and the patient may develop symptoms or measurable organ dysfunction. This chain illustrates why anatomy, physiology, pathology and clinical medicine are complementary rather than interchangeable disciplines.

3. Cellular adaptation and injury

Cells respond to altered demand or stress through adaptations such as hypertrophy (increased cell size), hyperplasia (increased cell number), atrophy (reduced cell size or tissue mass) and metaplasia (replacement of one differentiated cell type by another). Adaptation can be reversible, but persistent stress may exceed the cell's capacity to maintain homeostasis.

Cell injury may be reversible or irreversible. Causes include hypoxia and ischaemia, physical agents, chemicals and drugs, infectious agents, immune reactions, genetic defects and nutritional imbalance. Reversible injury can involve cellular swelling and fatty change in susceptible tissues. Severe or persistent injury can damage membranes, mitochondria and essential cellular processes, leading to cell death.

Necrosis is cell death associated with loss of membrane integrity and an inflammatory response. Apoptosis is a regulated form of cell death that usually involves controlled cellular dismantling and less surrounding inflammation. Other regulated cell-death pathways exist and may be important in particular diseases. The precise pattern depends on the cause, tissue and context.

4. Inflammation

Inflammation is a protective response to infection, tissue injury or harmful stimuli. It helps remove the cause of injury and initiates repair, but an excessive or persistent response can damage host tissues. Acute inflammation typically develops rapidly and involves changes in small blood vessels, fluid and protein movement into tissues, and recruitment of leukocytes—often neutrophils. Chronic inflammation lasts longer and may involve macrophages, lymphocytes, tissue destruction and simultaneous attempts at repair.

Classical local features are redness, heat, swelling, pain and reduced function, although not every feature is present in every case. Inflammation is not synonymous with infection: sterile injury can cause inflammation, while infection refers to invasion or multiplication of microorganisms. Diagnosis depends on context and appropriate evidence.

5. Tissue repair, regeneration and fibrosis

After injury, tissue may regenerate when viable cells can proliferate and the supporting framework remains adequate. Alternatively, healing may involve deposition of connective tissue and scar formation. Repair depends on the nature and extent of injury, tissue regenerative capacity, blood supply, infection, nutrition, mechanical stress and systemic factors such as diabetes or immune suppression.

Wound healing is often described in overlapping phases: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling. These are not perfectly separate stages. Excessive or poorly regulated repair can lead to fibrosis, contracture or abnormal scarring. In the nervous system and some other tissues, regenerative capacity is limited, so injury may have lasting functional consequences.

6. Haemodynamic disorders and shock

Oedema is excess fluid in interstitial tissues or body cavities. It may arise from increased hydrostatic pressure, reduced plasma oncotic pressure, increased vascular permeability, lymphatic obstruction or sodium and water retention. Hyperaemia is increased blood inflow due to active arteriolar dilation; congestion is impaired venous outflow and can produce tissue hypoxia.

Thrombosis is formation of a blood clot within the cardiovascular system. An embolus is material travelling through the circulation that can obstruct a vessel; it may be thrombotic or have another composition. Infarction is tissue necrosis caused by inadequate blood supply or, in some settings, impaired venous drainage. Shock is a state of circulatory failure in which tissue perfusion is inadequate; major categories include hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, distributive and obstructive shock. These conditions can be life-threatening and require urgent clinical assessment.

7. Immune-mediated disease and infection

Immune mechanisms protect against pathogens and abnormal cells, but dysregulated responses can cause disease. Hypersensitivity reactions are often grouped into four types for teaching purposes, although real diseases can involve overlapping mechanisms. Autoimmune disease arises when immune responses target self-components. Immunodeficiency increases susceptibility to particular infections and may affect tumour surveillance. Pathology also examines how microorganisms cause injury directly, through toxins, immune responses or disruption of normal tissue function.

8. Neoplasia: benign and malignant tumours

Neoplasia is abnormal clonal cell growth that is not adequately controlled by normal regulatory mechanisms. A benign tumour generally remains localised and does not metastasise, although its location or hormone production may still cause serious harm. A malignant tumour can invade surrounding tissues and may spread to distant sites. Malignancy is assessed through features such as differentiation, invasion, growth behaviour and metastatic potential, interpreted within the tumour type.

Cancer development often involves accumulated genetic and epigenetic alterations affecting growth, cell death, DNA repair, immune interaction and tissue environment. Grading describes aspects of tumour appearance or behaviour; staging describes the extent of disease. Their precise definitions and clinical use depend on tumour type and established classification systems. Neither should be inferred from one isolated microscopic feature.

9. How pathology connects with anatomy and physiology

  • Anatomy: identifies where a lesion is and which structures are affected.
  • Histology: shows the tissue and cellular patterns of injury.
  • Physiology: explains the normal function that has been disturbed.
  • Pathology: explains the disease mechanism and structural changes.
  • Clinical practice: integrates history, examination, laboratory tests, imaging and pathology to guide diagnosis and care.
  • Physiotherapy and rehabilitation: considers how disease or injury changes movement, activity, participation and recovery needs.

10. A disciplined approach to a pathology case

When studying a case, define the main clinical problem, identify the affected organ or tissue, describe the objective findings, and distinguish observations from interpretations. Ask what mechanisms could explain the findings, what alternatives remain possible, and which additional evidence would discriminate between them. Correlate gross, microscopic, laboratory and imaging findings when available. Do not assume that a single morphological pattern always has one cause.

11. Self-check questions

  1. Distinguish aetiology from pathogenesis.
  2. Explain the difference between hypertrophy and hyperplasia.
  3. Compare reversible cell injury, necrosis and apoptosis.
  4. Why is inflammation not synonymous with infection?
  5. Give two mechanisms that can cause oedema.
  6. Distinguish thrombosis, embolism and infarction.
  7. Explain the difference between tumour grading and staging.
  8. How can anatomy, physiology and pathology be combined to interpret one clinical problem?

12. Key terms

Aetiology: cause or contributing factors. Pathogenesis: mechanism and sequence of disease development. Morphology: structural appearance. Necrosis: cell death associated with membrane breakdown and inflammation. Apoptosis: regulated cell death. Fibrosis: excess deposition of connective tissue. Thrombosis: formation of a clot within a vessel or heart. Infarction: tissue necrosis from inadequate perfusion or drainage. Neoplasia: abnormal clonal growth.

Check your understanding

  1. What is the difference between aetiology and pathogenesis?
    Reveal answer

    Aetiology concerns the cause or origin of disease; pathogenesis describes the mechanisms through which disease develops.

  2. How does a sign differ from a symptom?
    Reveal answer

    A sign is objectively observed or measured; a symptom is experienced and reported by the patient.

  3. Why should morphology be interpreted alongside clinical history and laboratory findings?
    Reveal answer

    Different diseases can produce overlapping structural changes, and diagnosis depends on integrating morphology with clinical and other evidence.
