Spinal cord: segments, roots and spinal nerves

The spinal cord lies within the vertebral canal and connects the brain with the body through ascending sensory and descending motor pathways. In adults, it typically ends near the L1–L2 vertebral level as the conus medullaris, while spinal nerve roots continue inferiorly as the cauda equina. The exact level varies, and vertebral levels should not be confused with spinal cord segment levels. Thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves are formed by the union of posterior (dorsal) sensory roots and anterior (ventral) motor roots. The dorsal root ganglion contains sensory neuron cell bodies. After the mixed spinal nerve forms, it divides into posterior and anterior rami; both carry sensory and motor fibres. Posterior rami supply deep back muscles and overlying skin; anterior rami supply the anterolateral trunk and limbs and form major plexuses, except in much of the thoracic region where they continue as intercostal nerves. Spinal cord grey matter contains neuronal cell bodies and local circuits, while white matter contains myelinated and other axons in tracts. The central canal is a remnant of the neural tube lumen. Segmental organisation explains dermatomes, myotomes and reflex arcs, though overlap between adjacent segments is common.

Learning objectives

  • Describe spinal cord location and its adult termination level in general terms.
  • Distinguish dorsal roots, ventral roots, spinal nerves and rami.
  • Relate spinal segments to dermatomes, myotomes and reflexes.

High-yield study points

  • There are 31 pairs of spinal nerves.
  • Dorsal roots carry sensory fibres and contain dorsal root ganglia; ventral roots carry motor fibres.
  • A spinal nerve is mixed and divides into dorsal and ventral rami.
  • Dorsal rami supply deep back structures; ventral rami supply the anterolateral trunk and limbs.
  • Spinal cord segment levels do not exactly match vertebral levels, particularly inferiorly.

Clinical connection

Radicular pain, weakness and sensory change may follow a nerve-root distribution. Dermatome maps overlap and vary, so localisation uses the whole neurological examination.

Memory aid / mnemonic

DAVE = Dorsal Afferent, Ventral Efferent. For tract localisation, remember that dorsal-column fibres cross in the medulla while spinothalamic fibres cross in the spinal cord.

Check your understanding

  1. Where are the cell bodies of most primary somatic sensory neurons located?
    Reveal answer

    In dorsal root ganglia.

  2. What is the functional difference between dorsal and ventral roots of a typical spinal nerve?
    Reveal answer

    Dorsal roots carry sensory afferents into the spinal cord; ventral roots carry motor efferents out.

  3. What is a spinal cord segment?
    Reveal answer

    A portion of spinal cord associated with one pair of spinal nerves, defined by its root connections.

Deep study: spinal cord organisation and segmental logic

External landmarks and enlargement

The spinal cord continues from the medulla at the foramen magnum and usually ends as the conus medullaris near the L1–L2 vertebral level in adults, with normal variation. The lumbar and sacral roots descend within the dural sac as the cauda equina because the vertebral column grows longer than the cord during development. The cervical enlargement supplies the upper limb; the lumbosacral enlargement supplies the lower limb. The filum terminale helps anchor the cord inferiorly.

Thirty-one spinal nerve pairs

There are 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral and 1 coccygeal pairs. Each typical spinal nerve forms when a posterior (dorsal) sensory root joins an anterior (ventral) motor root. The dorsal root ganglion contains sensory neuron cell bodies; it is not the synapse for the usual first-order sensory neuron. After a short mixed spinal nerve, dorsal and ventral rami both carry sensory and motor fibres. Dorsal rami supply intrinsic back muscles and overlying skin; ventral rami supply the anterolateral trunk and limbs, forming plexuses except in most thoracic segments.

Grey matter and white matter

The posterior horn processes incoming sensory information; the anterior horn contains somatic motor neurons for skeletal muscle; the lateral horn is prominent in thoracolumbar sympathetic outflow (classically T1–L2) and sacral parasympathetic neurons arise from S2–S4. The central canal contains cerebrospinal fluid and is lined by ependymal cells. White matter is organised into posterior, lateral and anterior funiculi containing ascending, descending and propriospinal fibres. The relative amount of white matter generally increases rostrally as more long tracts join the cord.

Dermatomes, myotomes and reflex arcs

A dermatome is an area of skin predominantly supplied by one spinal root, but adjacent dermatomes overlap. A myotome describes muscle action predominantly linked to a spinal segment. Useful clinical landmarks include C6 (thumb region), C7 (middle finger), C8 (little finger), T4 (approximately nipple line), T10 (approximately umbilicus), L4 (medial leg) and S1 (lateral foot); these are approximate and vary. A reflex arc includes receptor, afferent neuron, central synapse or integration, efferent neuron and effector. The patellar reflex chiefly tests L3–L4 (often L4 emphasis), while the Achilles reflex chiefly tests S1–S2 (often S1 emphasis).

Why cord lesions produce patterned deficits

Brown-Séquard-type hemicord injury can produce ipsilateral corticospinal weakness below the lesion and ipsilateral loss of vibration/position sense, with contralateral pain-temperature loss beginning a few segments below because spinothalamic fibres cross near entry. A central cord lesion may affect crossing pain-temperature fibres near the anterior white commissure; if it expands, other tracts may be involved. Anterior spinal artery territory injury can affect anterior two-thirds of the cord, often impairing motor function and pain-temperature while relatively sparing dorsal-column modalities. Real lesions are rarely perfectly textbook-shaped.

Self-test: explain, do not only memorise

  1. Why are lumbar punctures usually performed below the adult conus medullaris?
    Reveal reasoning

    Commonly at L3–L4 or L4–L5, where the cord has usually ended and the lumbar cistern contains mobile nerve roots in CSF. The procedure must be performed by trained clinicians using appropriate landmarks and contraindication checks.

  2. Does a ventral ramus contain only motor fibres?
    Reveal answer

    No. Dorsal and ventral rami are mixed, carrying motor, sensory and associated autonomic fibres.

  3. Why can pain-temperature loss begin a little below a spinal cord lesion?
    Reveal answer

    Primary afferents may ascend or descend briefly in Lissauer’s tract, and second-order fibres cross over a short rostrocaudal distance through the anterior white commissure.
