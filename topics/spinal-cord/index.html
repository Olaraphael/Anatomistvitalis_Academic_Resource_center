Spinal cord: segments, roots and spinal nerves

The spinal cord lies within the vertebral canal and connects the brain with the body through ascending sensory and descending motor pathways. In adults, it typically ends near the L1–L2 vertebral level as the conus medullaris, while spinal nerve roots continue inferiorly as the cauda equina. The exact level varies, and vertebral levels should not be confused with spinal cord segment levels. Thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves are formed by the union of posterior (dorsal) sensory roots and anterior (ventral) motor roots. The dorsal root ganglion contains sensory neuron cell bodies. After the mixed spinal nerve forms, it divides into posterior and anterior rami; both carry sensory and motor fibres. Posterior rami supply deep back muscles and overlying skin; anterior rami supply the anterolateral trunk and limbs and form major plexuses, except in much of the thoracic region where they continue as intercostal nerves. Spinal cord grey matter contains neuronal cell bodies and local circuits, while white matter contains myelinated and other axons in tracts. The central canal is a remnant of the neural tube lumen. Segmental organisation explains dermatomes, myotomes and reflex arcs, though overlap between adjacent segments is common.

Learning objectives

  • Describe spinal cord location and its adult termination level in general terms.
  • Distinguish dorsal roots, ventral roots, spinal nerves and rami.
  • Relate spinal segments to dermatomes, myotomes and reflexes.

High-yield study points

  • There are 31 pairs of spinal nerves.
  • Dorsal roots carry sensory fibres and contain dorsal root ganglia; ventral roots carry motor fibres.
  • A spinal nerve is mixed and divides into dorsal and ventral rami.
  • Dorsal rami supply deep back structures; ventral rami supply the anterolateral trunk and limbs.
  • Spinal cord segment levels do not exactly match vertebral levels, particularly inferiorly.

Clinical connection

Radicular pain, weakness and sensory change may follow a nerve-root distribution. Dermatome maps overlap and vary, so localisation uses the whole neurological examination.
