Directional terms and anatomical body regions

Regional anatomy divides the body into named areas so structures can be located consistently. Regional terms are paired with directional terms to create exact descriptions. The axial region includes the head, neck and trunk; the appendicular regions include the upper and lower limbs and their girdles. Names may vary slightly between teaching traditions, so learn the standard term and recognise common synonyms.

High-yield study points

  • Cephalic refers to the head; cranial to the skull; facial to the face; cervical to the neck.
  • Thoracic refers to the chest; sternal to the breastbone; pectoral to the chest region; mammary to the breast.
  • Abdominal refers to the abdomen; umbilical to the navel; pelvic to the pelvis; inguinal to the groin; perineal to the region between the external genitalia and anus.
  • Dorsal or back regions include vertebral, scapular, lumbar, sacral and gluteal regions.
  • In the upper limb, brachial is the arm between shoulder and elbow, antebrachial is the forearm, carpal is the wrist, palmar is the palm and digital is the fingers.
  • In the lower limb, femoral is the thigh, patellar is the anterior knee, popliteal is the posterior knee, crural is the leg between knee and ankle, tarsal is the ankle and plantar is the sole.
  • The anatomical 'arm' is not the whole upper limb; it is the segment from shoulder to elbow. The 'leg' is the segment from knee to ankle, not the entire lower limb.
  • Regional naming is often combined with side and depth, for example 'superficial structure in the right medial thigh'.

Clinical connection

Regional terminology supports physical examination, surgery, physiotherapy and documentation. For abdominal pain, region names can help communicate location but cannot independently establish a diagnosis.
