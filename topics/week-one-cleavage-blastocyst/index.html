Week 1: cleavage, morula and blastocyst

16 min studyFree learning resourceIncludes memory aid

Lesson overview

After fertilisation, cleavage produces repeated mitotic divisions without a proportional increase in the overall size of the conceptus. Blastomeres compact to form a morula; fluid then creates a blastocyst cavity. The blastocyst contains an inner cell mass (embryoblast), which forms the embryo proper, and an outer trophoblast, which contributes to fetal components of the placenta. The blastocyst sheds the zona pellucida before implantation begins, usually around days 6–7 after fertilisation.

Learning objectives

  • Describe cleavage and compaction.
  • Distinguish morula from blastocyst.
  • Identify embryoblast and trophoblast.

High-yield points

  • Cleavage partitions the original cytoplasm into smaller blastomeres.
  • Compaction occurs around the 8-cell stage.
  • The blastocyst contains a cavity, embryoblast and trophoblast.
  • Hatching from the zona pellucida allows implantation to begin.

Memory aid / mnemonic

Week 1: “Divide → Compact → Cavitate → Hatch.” Morula = solid ball; blastocyst = fluid-filled cavity with inner cell mass.

Study tip: hide this section, explain the concept aloud, then check whether you remembered the sequence and the reason behind it.

Clinical connection

Abnormal cleavage or implantation may contribute to early pregnancy loss; embryo grading does not guarantee a pregnancy outcome.

Week one: follow the changing conceptus

After fertilisation, the zygote undergoes cleavage as it moves along the uterine tube. Each mitotic division produces smaller blastomeres without a major increase in the overall size enclosed by the zona pellucida. Compaction makes the blastomeres adhere closely, helping establish outer and inner cell populations.

The morula is a compact ball of cells. Fluid enters to form the blastocyst cavity, creating a blastocyst with three key components: trophoblast (outer cell layer), embryoblast (inner cell mass) and blastocyst cavity. The embryoblast gives rise to the embryo and some extraembryonic tissues; trophoblast contributes to the fetal placenta.

Before implantation, the blastocyst must hatch from the zona pellucida. Implantation begins around day 6–7 after fertilisation, although timing varies. Trophoblast differentiates into cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast, which interact with and invade the endometrial lining in a regulated process.

Common confusions to avoid

  • The morula is not yet the blastocyst: a blastocyst has a fluid-filled cavity and distinct inner and outer cell populations.
  • Cleavage increases cell number but initially does not greatly increase conceptus size.
  • The trophoblast is not the whole placenta; it contributes to the fetal component, which works with maternal tissues.
  • Implantation is a process, not a single instantaneous event.

Quick self-test

  1. What is the key structural feature distinguishing the blastocyst from the morula?
  2. Which blastocyst population forms the embryo proper?
  3. Why is cleavage not associated with a large increase in overall size?
Reveal answers and explanations

1. The fluid-filled blastocyst cavity and differentiated inner/outer populations. 2. The embryoblast (inner cell mass). 3. Divisions partition the existing cytoplasm within the zona pellucida before substantial growth.
