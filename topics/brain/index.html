Cerebral lobes and functions

The cerebral cortex supports perception, voluntary movement, language, memory, attention and executive function. Functional regions are distributed across lobes and connected networks; functions should not be reduced to isolated one-lobe explanations.

Learning objectives

  • Identify the cerebrum, diencephalon, brainstem and cerebellum as major brain divisions.
  • Relate cerebral lobes and selected cortical regions to broad functions.
  • Explain how white matter, grey matter and major commissural pathways organise communication.
  • Connect gross brain anatomy to basic clinical localisation without assuming one symptom always identifies one lesion.

High-yield study points

  • Frontal lobe: motor planning, voluntary movement and executive functions; dominant hemisphere regions contribute to speech production.
  • Parietal lobe: somatic sensation, spatial processing and sensory integration.
  • Temporal lobe: hearing, memory and aspects of language comprehension.
  • Occipital lobe: visual processing.
  • The insula contributes to taste, interoception and integration of internal bodily states.

Clinical connection

Stroke symptoms depend on the vascular territory and networks affected. Weakness, sensory loss, language difficulty or visual field loss can help localise a lesion.

Memory aid / mnemonic

Frontal = planning and voluntary movement; parietal = body sensation and spatial integration; temporal = hearing and memory; occipital = vision. Cue: Plan, Perceive, Process sound, Observe.

Revision tip: recall the cue without looking, then explain the anatomical reason behind it.

Check your understanding

  1. Which major division coordinates movement and contributes to balance and motor learning?
    Reveal answer

    The cerebellum.

  2. What is the corpus callosum?
    Reveal answer

    A large commissural white-matter tract connecting the two cerebral hemispheres.

  3. Why is a brain lesion localised using a pattern of findings rather than one symptom alone?
    Reveal answer

    Many functions involve distributed networks, and lesion effects depend on exact location, size, timing and surrounding pathways.

Deep study: cerebral cortex, white matter and functional networks

Surface anatomy and sulcal landmarks

The cerebral hemispheres are separated by the longitudinal fissure and connected mainly by commissural fibres, especially the corpus callosum. The central sulcus separates the precentral gyrus of the frontal lobe from the postcentral gyrus of the parietal lobe. The lateral sulcus overlies the insula and separates much of the temporal lobe from frontal and parietal regions. The parieto-occipital sulcus is a prominent medial landmark. Surface landmarks are guides: functional boundaries vary and should not be inferred from a single gyrus alone.

Functional localisation with network thinking

The primary motor cortex (Brodmann area 4) lies mainly in the precentral gyrus; the primary somatosensory cortex (areas 3, 1 and 2) lies mainly in the postcentral gyrus. Their body representations are somatotopic: the medial surface represents much of the lower limb, while the lateral convexity represents face and upper limb. This is a distorted map, not a uniform miniature body. The premotor and supplementary motor areas help prepare movement; posterior parietal cortex integrates sensory information for spatially guided action.

The dominant hemisphere—usually the left—contains major language networks in most people. Inferior frontal regions contribute to speech production and language planning, while posterior temporal and temporoparietal regions contribute to comprehension and mapping sound to meaning. Language depends on distributed, connected networks, not two isolated “centres”. The non-dominant hemisphere contributes strongly to visuospatial attention, prosody and aspects of social communication.

Association cortex and limbic structures

Prefrontal association cortex supports working memory, planning, inhibition, decision-making and flexible behaviour. The hippocampal formation is important for forming new episodic memories; the amygdala participates in assigning emotional significance and learning from threat or reward. The cingulate cortex links cognition, motivation and autonomic responses. These functions emerge from networks and connections rather than single structures acting alone.

White matter: the connection map

Association fibres connect cortical areas within one hemisphere (for example, the superior longitudinal fasciculus); commissural fibres connect the hemispheres (especially the corpus callosum); projection fibres connect cortex with subcortical structures, brainstem and spinal cord (including the corona radiata and internal capsule). A small internal-capsule lesion can produce dense contralateral weakness because many descending fibres are tightly packed there.

Blood supply and clinical reasoning

The middle cerebral artery commonly supplies much of the lateral frontal, parietal and temporal cortex, including face/arm motor-sensory regions and dominant-hemisphere language networks. The anterior cerebral artery supplies much of the medial frontal and parietal surface, including lower-limb representation. The posterior cerebral artery supplies occipital visual cortex and inferomedial temporal regions. These are broad patterns: vascular territories vary and stroke syndromes depend on the exact vessel, collateral flow and lesion size.

Clinical localisation drill

  1. A patient has right facial and arm weakness with expressive language difficulty. Which hemisphere and likely arterial territory should be considered first?
    Reveal reasoning

    The left dominant hemisphere is likely, with a possible left middle cerebral artery territory lesion. This is a localisation exercise, not a diagnosis; acute deficits require emergency assessment.

  2. A lesion selectively affecting the medial frontal motor region is most likely to produce weakness that is prominent in which body region?
    Reveal answer

    The contralateral lower limb, because lower-limb motor representation lies medially near the paracentral lobule.

  3. How do association, commissural and projection fibres differ?
    Reveal answer

    Association fibres link regions within one hemisphere; commissural fibres cross between hemispheres; projection fibres link cortex with lower or deeper nervous-system levels.
