Homeostasis and feedback

Homeostasis is the regulation of the internal environment within ranges compatible with normal cellular function. It is dynamic rather than perfectly constant. Receptors detect change, control centres integrate information, and effectors produce responses.

Learning objectives

  • Define the internal environment and explain why extracellular fluid is central to homeostasis.
  • Distinguish regulated variables from the mechanisms that control them.
  • Compare negative and positive feedback with examples.
  • Explain how multiple organ systems cooperate to maintain temperature, blood glucose, fluid balance and pH.

High-yield study points

  • Negative feedback opposes an initial change and is the most common homeostatic pattern.
  • Positive feedback amplifies a process until a defined endpoint, as in labour contractions.
  • Core regulated variables include temperature, blood glucose, pH and extracellular fluid composition.
  • A physiological set point or operating range can vary with context and time.

Clinical connection

Fever reflects a regulated rise in hypothalamic temperature set point; it differs mechanistically from uncontrolled hyperthermia.

Check your understanding

  1. Which fluid compartment is often called the internal environment of the body's cells?
    Reveal answer

    Extracellular fluid.

  2. How does negative feedback respond to a deviation from a regulated range?
    Reveal answer

    It activates responses that oppose the deviation and tend to return the variable toward its regulated range.

  3. Why is homeostasis dynamic rather than perfectly constant?
    Reveal answer

    Variables fluctuate within ranges and control systems continuously adjust to changing demands and conditions.

Check your understanding

  1. Why is negative feedback not the same as “bad” feedback?
    Answer and explanation

    Negative feedback opposes a deviation from a regulated value; it is a stabilising mechanism, such as thermoregulation or blood-glucose control.

  2. How does positive feedback differ?
    Answer and explanation

    Positive feedback amplifies a process until a terminating event occurs, as in the amplification of uterine contractions during labour or the clotting cascade.

  3. Why is a set point not always a fixed number?
    Answer and explanation

    Some regulated values vary with circadian rhythms, activity, age and physiological state; control operates around an appropriate range rather than one universal constant.

Components of a homeostatic control system

  1. Regulated variable: the quantity maintained within an appropriate range, such as core temperature, plasma glucose, osmolality or arterial pressure.
  2. Sensor/receptor: detects a change in the variable or a related signal.
  3. Afferent communication: carries information to an integrating centre when the system uses a distinct neural or hormonal pathway.
  4. Integrating centre: compares information with a target range and coordinates a response.
  5. Efferent signal and effectors: nerves, hormones or local signals alter the activity of tissues that influence the variable.
  6. Feedback: the response changes the original stimulus and influences subsequent output.

Examples across systems

  • Thermoregulation: thermosensitive pathways in the nervous system coordinate sweating, skin blood flow, shivering and behaviour.
  • Blood glucose: pancreatic islet hormones and target tissues regulate uptake, storage and production of glucose; the system also depends on food intake, activity and stress.
  • Osmolality: hypothalamic osmoregulatory mechanisms, thirst, ADH and renal water handling work together to regulate body-fluid concentration.
  • Arterial pressure: baroreceptor reflexes act rapidly, while kidneys, hormones and volume regulation contribute to longer-term control.

Negative feedback, positive feedback and feedforward

Negative feedback opposes a deviation and stabilises a variable. Positive feedback amplifies a process until a defined endpoint, such as the clotting cascade or labour contractions. Feedforward control anticipates a predictable demand—for example, changes in cardiovascular and respiratory activity at the start of exercise—before the full disturbance occurs. Real physiology combines these mechanisms rather than relying on a single loop.

Homeostasis is dynamic

Many variables fluctuate within ranges and change with sleep, exercise, meals, age, circadian rhythm and reproductive state. A measured value should be interpreted with timing and context. A set point is not necessarily a single immutable number, and the body may prioritise one variable at the expense of another during stress or disease.
