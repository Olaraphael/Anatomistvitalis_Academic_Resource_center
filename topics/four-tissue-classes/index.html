The four basic tissue classes

Most organs combine four basic tissue classes: epithelium, connective tissue, muscle tissue and nervous tissue. Each class is defined by a characteristic organisation and function, but real organs are integrated structures in which these tissues communicate and depend on one another.

High-yield study points

  • Epithelial tissue covers surfaces, lines cavities and forms glands; cells are closely packed, polarised and supported by a basement membrane.
  • Connective tissue supports, binds, protects and stores energy; cells are distributed within an extracellular matrix containing fibres and ground substance.
  • Muscle tissue contracts to generate force; skeletal muscle is usually voluntary and striated, cardiac muscle is striated and involuntary, and smooth muscle is non-striated and involuntary.
  • Nervous tissue contains neurons and supporting glial cells that receive, process and transmit information.
  • Most epithelia are avascular and receive nutrients by diffusion from underlying connective tissue; connective tissue often contains vessels and nerves.
  • Extracellular matrix is not merely filler: its composition affects tissue strength, elasticity, cell signalling and repair.
  • Organs contain combinations of all four classes, for example the gut wall with epithelium, connective tissue, smooth muscle and enteric nerves.

Clinical connection

Tissue class and architecture help predict function and injury response. A superficial epithelial abrasion differs from damage to a vascular connective tissue or a contractile muscle.
