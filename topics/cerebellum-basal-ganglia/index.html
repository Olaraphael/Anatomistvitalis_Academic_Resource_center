Cerebellum and basal ganglia: coordination and movement selection

24 min studyFree learning resourceIncludes memory aid

Lesson overview

The cerebellum compares intended movement with sensory feedback to calibrate timing, precision, balance and motor learning. It connects with brainstem and cerebral cortex through three paired peduncles. Lesions commonly cause ipsilateral ataxia, dysmetria, intention tremor, nystagmus or gait instability. Basal ganglia circuits—including striatum, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus and substantia nigra—help select and scale actions through loops with cortex and thalamus. Dopamine from substantia nigra pars compacta modulates striatal circuits. Different circuit disorders can produce bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, chorea or dystonia.

Learning objectives

  • Contrast cerebellar coordination with basal ganglia action selection.
  • Identify major structures in each system.
  • Relate broad movement signs to system dysfunction.

High-yield points

  • Cerebellar limb signs are commonly ipsilateral due to the organisation of its connections.
  • Cerebellum supports error correction and motor learning.
  • Basal ganglia direct/indirect pathway diagrams are simplified models of complex networks.
  • Movement abnormalities have multiple causes and require clinical interpretation.

Memory aid / mnemonic

Clinical connection

Ataxia, tremor, rigidity and involuntary movements can arise from many neurological or systemic conditions; the pattern guides assessment but is not a diagnosis alone.

Cerebellar cortex and output

The cerebellar cortex has three layers: an outer molecular layer containing Purkinje dendrites and interneurons; a single row of large Purkinje cell bodies; and a densely cellular granular layer. Purkinje cells are the sole output neurons of the cerebellar cortex and are inhibitory (GABAergic). They project to deep cerebellar nuclei and, in vestibulocerebellar circuits, to vestibular nuclei. Mossy fibres convey diverse information and excite granule cells; granule-cell parallel fibres excite Purkinje cells. Climbing fibres from the inferior olivary complex provide powerful teaching-related input. The deep nuclei integrate cortical inhibition with excitatory collaterals and provide much of cerebellar output.

Functional divisions

The vestibulocerebellum (flocculonodular lobe and related circuits) contributes to balance and gaze stabilisation. The spinocerebellum (vermis and intermediate zone) monitors ongoing movement and posture; the vermis is strongly linked to axial/proximal control, while the intermediate zone helps coordinate distal limb movements. The cerebrocerebellum (lateral hemispheres) contributes to planning, timing, motor learning and cognitive processes through loops with cerebral cortex. These are functional networks, not isolated boxes.

Peduncles and lesion patterns

The inferior cerebellar peduncle carries substantial spinal, vestibular and olivary input; the middle peduncle carries pontocerebellar input from the opposite cerebral hemisphere’s cortical plans; the superior peduncle is a major output route, with many fibres crossing in the midbrain. Cerebellar lesions usually cause ipsilateral limb dysmetria, intention tremor, decomposition of movement, dysdiadochokinesia or ataxia because cerebellar output pathways cross in a way that results in predominantly ipsilateral body coordination effects. Vermian disease may cause truncal or gait ataxia; flocculonodular dysfunction can cause imbalance and nystagmus.

Basal ganglia: selecting and scaling actions

The striatum (caudate and putamen) receives widespread cortical input. The direct pathway, involving striatal inhibition of GPi/SNr output neurons, can reduce tonic inhibition of thalamocortical motor circuits and facilitate a selected action. The indirect pathway, through GPe and subthalamic nucleus, tends to increase inhibitory basal-ganglia output and suppress competing actions. The hyperdirect pathway sends rapid cortical input to the subthalamic nucleus. Dopamine from substantia nigra pars compacta modulates D1- and D2-expressing striatal populations; the simplified teaching model is useful but real circuits include parallel loops and multiple transmitters.

Parkinson disease is associated with degeneration of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons and can produce bradykinesia, rigidity and resting tremor. Huntington disease involves degeneration within striatal circuits and may cause chorea and cognitive/psychiatric change. Cerebellar disease primarily disrupts timing and error correction, while basal-ganglia disease often disrupts movement initiation, scaling and selection; this is a useful distinction, not an absolute rule.

Applied self-test

  1. Which cerebellar cortical cell provides the cortex’s principal output?
    Answer

    The Purkinje cell, which is inhibitory and projects to deep cerebellar or vestibular nuclei.

  2. A patient overshoots a target with the left hand and has intention tremor. Which side of cerebellar involvement is most likely?
    Answer

    The left cerebellar hemisphere is a strong possibility because limb coordination deficits are typically ipsilateral. Clinical assessment must consider other causes.

  3. What is the broad contrast between cerebellar and basal-ganglia dysfunction?
    Answer

    Cerebellar disorders often impair coordination, timing and error correction; basal-ganglia disorders often affect movement selection, initiation and scaling.
