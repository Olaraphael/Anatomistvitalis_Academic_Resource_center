Digestive physiology: motility, secretion, digestion and absorption

The digestive system receives food, mechanically and chemically breaks it down, absorbs useful molecules and water, and eliminates residual material. Motility, secretion, digestion and absorption are coordinated by enteric neural circuits, autonomic inputs, hormones and local signals. Different regions specialise in different tasks, and normal function depends on the integrity of mucosa, blood supply and coordinated movement.

High-yield study points

  • Mastication and gastric mixing mechanically reduce food particles and mix them with secretions.
  • Salivary and pancreatic enzymes begin or continue digestion of carbohydrates, lipids and proteins; the enzyme and pH environment differ by region.
  • Gastric acid helps denature proteins and supports pepsin activity, while mucus and bicarbonate protect the gastric mucosa.
  • Bile salts emulsify dietary lipids and support micelle formation; they are not digestive enzymes themselves.
  • Most nutrient absorption occurs in the small intestine, whose folds, villi and microvilli increase absorptive surface area.
  • The colon absorbs water and electrolytes, houses a complex microbiota and compacts residual material into faeces.
  • Enteric neural circuits coordinate peristalsis and segmentation; hormones such as gastrin, secretin and cholecystokinin modulate digestive activity.

Clinical connection

Symptoms such as abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhoea or constipation can arise from many mechanisms. Anatomy, motility, secretion, absorption and systemic factors all contribute to clinical reasoning.

Digestion: make food absorbable

The digestive system performs motility, secretion, digestion, absorption and elimination. Mechanical processing increases surface area; enzymes hydrolyse macromolecules into smaller units that can cross the intestinal epithelium. Carbohydrates are absorbed mainly as monosaccharides, proteins as amino acids and small peptides, and lipids largely as fatty acids and monoglycerides carried in micelles before entering enterocytes.

Control of secretion and motility

Enteric neural circuits coordinate local responses, while parasympathetic and sympathetic input modulate them. Gastrin supports gastric acid secretion and mucosal growth; secretin stimulates bicarbonate-rich pancreatic and biliary secretion in response to acid; cholecystokinin promotes pancreatic enzyme secretion and gallbladder contraction in response to fats and amino acids. These hormones coordinate different parts of digestion rather than acting as interchangeable “digestive hormones.”

Absorption patterns

  • Small intestine: primary site for most nutrient absorption, supported by folds, villi and microvilli.
  • Bile salts: emulsify dietary fat and assist micelle formation; they are largely reabsorbed in the terminal ileum.
  • Colon: absorbs water and electrolytes and houses microbiota that metabolise some unabsorbed substrates.

Clinical reasoning

Diarrhoea can result from osmotic retention of water, secretion, inflammation or altered transit; different mechanisms require different evaluation. Pancreatic insufficiency can impair digestion of fat and protein, while terminal ileal disease may interfere with bile-salt recycling and vitamin B12 absorption. These mechanisms explain symptoms but do not diagnose an individual patient.

Self-test

  1. Which hormone promotes bicarbonate-rich secretion in response to acid?
  2. Where does most nutrient absorption occur?
  3. What is the role of bile salts in fat digestion and absorption?
Answers with reasoning

1. Secretin. 2. Small intestine. 3. They aid emulsification and micelle formation, helping lipid digestion products reach the enterocyte surface.
