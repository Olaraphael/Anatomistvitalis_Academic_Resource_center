Clinical anatomy: applying structure to symptoms and examination

Clinical anatomy applies knowledge of location, relationships, innervation, blood supply and normal variation to understand examination findings, injury patterns and diagnostic procedures. A structured approach begins with the region and laterality, then identifies relevant surface landmarks, likely tissues, neurovascular structures and anatomical pathways. Findings must be interpreted in the context of history, physiology, examination reliability and appropriate investigations. Anatomical relationships support reasoning but rarely establish a diagnosis alone; referred pain, overlapping innervation and individual variation can make patterns less straightforward than textbook diagrams suggest.

Learning objectives

  • Use a systematic region-based approach to clinical anatomy.
  • Explain why laterality and precise localisation matter.
  • Distinguish anatomical reasoning from definitive diagnosis.

High-yield study points

  • Always document side, location, time course and relevant associated findings clearly.
  • Dermatomes, peripheral nerve territories and referred pain patterns are related but not interchangeable.
  • Anatomical landmarks guide examination and procedures but may vary with body habitus and individual anatomy.
  • Clinical decisions combine anatomy with history, physiology, tests and professional judgement.
  • Use current guidance and escalate urgent red flags rather than relying on a single anatomical clue.

Clinical connection

Sudden neurological deficit, severe chest pain, major trauma, breathing difficulty or rapidly worsening symptoms require urgent medical care. Educational anatomy is not a substitute for clinical assessment.
