Resting membrane potential

The resting membrane potential arises from unequal ion distributions across the cell membrane and selective permeability, especially to potassium in many neurons. The sodium-potassium ATPase maintains gradients over time but is not the sole immediate cause of the resting voltage.

High-yield study points

  • Potassium concentration is generally higher inside cells than outside.
  • At rest, many neuronal membranes are more permeable to potassium than sodium.
  • Potassium movement through leak channels contributes strongly to resting potential.
  • The sodium-potassium ATPase moves three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in per ATP hydrolysed.
  • An action potential is a rapid, transient change in membrane potential dependent on voltage-gated ion channels.

Clinical connection

Electrolyte disturbances and drugs that alter ion channels can change excitability and conduction.

Memory aid / mnemonic

At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open for rapid depolarisation; K⁺ efflux supports repolarisation. Channel timing and refractory periods shape the signal.

Neuron structure linked to function

A neuron receives, integrates and transmits information. Dendrites and the cell body receive and integrate many synaptic inputs; the axon initial segment is a common site of action-potential initiation; the axon conducts signals; and axon terminals release neurotransmitter at synapses. This is a useful model, although neurons vary considerably in shape and signalling pattern.

Most neurons maintain a resting membrane potential through selective membrane permeability, ion gradients and the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. The pump uses ATP to move three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in per cycle, maintaining gradients over time. Ion channels, rather than the pump alone, generate the rapid voltage changes of an action potential.

Action potential in sequence

  1. A stimulus depolarises the membrane toward threshold.
  2. Voltage-gated sodium channels open, producing rapid depolarisation.
  3. Sodium channels inactivate and voltage-gated potassium conductance increases.
  4. Potassium efflux repolarises the membrane; a brief after-hyperpolarisation may follow.
  5. Refractory periods help ensure forward propagation and limit firing frequency.

In myelinated axons, action potentials are regenerated at nodes of Ranvier, allowing saltatory conduction. Demyelination slows or blocks conduction because current leaks and nodal signalling becomes less efficient.

Synapses and neurotransmission

At a typical chemical synapse, an arriving action potential opens presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium entry triggers vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release. Transmitter binds postsynaptic receptors, changing ion conductance or activating intracellular signalling. The effect may be excitatory or inhibitory depending on receptor and ionic context—not simply on the transmitter name alone. Signal termination may involve reuptake, enzymatic breakdown or diffusion.

Electrical synapses pass current through gap junctions and can synchronise activity. Chemical synapses are more common in many neural circuits and allow modulation and plasticity.

Quick self-test

  1. Where are action potentials commonly initiated?
  2. Which ion movement produces the rapid upstroke of a typical neuronal action potential?
  3. Why does myelin increase conduction speed?
Reveal answers and explanations

1. The axon initial segment. 2. Sodium influx through voltage-gated sodium channels. 3. It limits current loss and allows action potentials to be regenerated at nodes of Ranvier.
