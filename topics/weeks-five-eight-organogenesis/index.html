Weeks 5–8: organogenesis and external form

20 min studyFree learning resourceIncludes memory aid

Lesson overview

Weeks 5–8 after fertilisation are a period of rapid organogenesis and shaping. The brain expands and flexes, facial prominences grow and fuse, limb buds develop into recognisable segments, and the heart, gut, kidneys and other organs undergo major structural changes. Pharyngeal arches contribute to head and neck structures; limb rotation establishes adult-like orientation; the midgut temporarily herniates into the umbilical cord before returning to the abdominal cavity. By the end of week 8, the basic plan of most major organ systems is established, although growth and maturation continue throughout fetal life.

Learning objectives

  • Identify major patterns of organogenesis.
  • Explain stage-dependent vulnerability to teratogens.
  • Distinguish organ formation from later maturation.

High-yield points

  • The embryonic period is a major window for many structural malformations.
  • Limb buds undergo elongation, segmentation and rotation.
  • The primitive gut develops foregut, midgut and hindgut regions.
  • The “all-or-none” rule for very early exposure is simplified and not a guarantee for an individual pregnancy.

Memory aid / mnemonic

Clinical connection

Effects of medicines, infections, alcohol and other exposures depend on timing, dose and context. Seek personalised advice for a possible pregnancy exposure.

Check your understanding

  1. Why are weeks three to eight after fertilisation especially important for structural development?
    Reveal answer

    This is the principal embryonic period of organogenesis, when major organ systems are established and many structures are particularly vulnerable to disruptive exposures.

  2. Does every organ complete development by the end of week eight?
    Reveal answer

    No. Major organ-system primordia are established, but growth, differentiation and functional maturation continue during the fetal period and after birth.

  3. Why should developmental age be stated clearly?
    Reveal answer

    Post-fertilisation age and gestational age differ by roughly two weeks in typical dating, so confusing them can misstate developmental timing.

Weeks five to eight: organogenesis in context

During this period, organ primordia formed earlier become more recognisable and the embryo undergoes rapid changes in body shape. Development is not a series of isolated events: growth, tissue interactions, folding, rotation, migration and programmed cell death occur in coordinated sequences.

Major developmental themes

  • Head and face: rapid growth of the brain changes head proportions. Facial prominences develop and merge through tightly coordinated growth; failure of normal fusion can produce clefting anomalies.
  • Limb development: limb buds form, elongate and develop regional patterning. Apical ectodermal ridge signalling supports proximodistal growth, while other signalling centres help establish anteroposterior and dorsoventral pattern.
  • Heart and circulation: the primitive heart tube loops and internal partitions develop. The timing and sequence of septation and outflow-tract development are important for understanding congenital cardiac defects.
  • Gut and body wall: embryonic folding incorporates part of the yolk sac into the primitive gut tube and brings ventral body-wall structures toward the midline.
  • Neural development: the brain vesicles and spinal cord continue to differentiate; neural crest cells migrate to form diverse structures, including components of the peripheral nervous system.
  • Urogenital development: early renal and gonadal structures develop in sequence, and reproductive tract differentiation continues through interactions among tissues and signalling pathways.

Embryonic period versus fetal period

By the end of week eight after fertilisation, the basic body plan and the primordia of major organ systems are established, although organs remain immature. From week nine after fertilisation, the fetal period is characterised mainly by growth, maturation and refinement. The transition is a useful teaching convention rather than a claim that all organ development stops at week eight.

Clinical reasoning

Teratogenic susceptibility depends on the developmental process occurring at the time of exposure. The same exposure may affect different structures at different times, and not every exposure produces an anomaly. Interpret risk using current evidence and specialist guidance rather than a single simplified timeline.
