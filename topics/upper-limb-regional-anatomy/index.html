Regional anatomy of the upper limb

The upper limb is organised into the pectoral girdle, axilla, arm, cubital fossa, forearm, wrist and hand. The clavicle and scapula connect the limb to the axial skeleton functionally, while the humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges provide the framework for movement. The brachial plexus supplies most motor and sensory innervation. The axillary artery continues as the brachial artery, which divides into radial and ulnar arteries; superficial and deep venous pathways return blood. Flexor compartments are generally anterior and extensor compartments posterior, with important regional exceptions and shared neurovascular structures.

Learning objectives

  • Name the main regions and bones of the upper limb.
  • Describe the broad organisation of the brachial plexus and major vessels.
  • Relate compartments to movements and common nerve injuries.

High-yield study points

  • The axilla is a passageway for major vessels, brachial plexus cords and lymph nodes.
  • The musculocutaneous nerve mainly supplies anterior arm flexors; the radial nerve supplies most extensor compartments.
  • The median and ulnar nerves are especially important in forearm and hand function.
  • The anatomical snuffbox overlies the scaphoid; tenderness after a fall can indicate fracture.
  • Hand function depends on coordinated extrinsic and intrinsic muscles plus sensory feedback.

Clinical connection

A humeral shaft fracture may injure the radial nerve; surgical-neck fractures may threaten the axillary nerve. Assessments integrate motor function, sensation, pulses and the injury mechanism.

Check your understanding

  1. Which nerve is commonly associated with the surgical neck of the humerus?
    Reveal answer

    The axillary nerve, which also supplies the deltoid and teres minor.

  2. Which major artery is palpated at the wrist on the lateral side in anatomical position?
    Reveal answer

    The radial artery.

  3. What is the main clinical value of knowing the brachial plexus organisation?
    Reveal answer

    It helps relate patterns of weakness and sensory loss to roots, trunks, divisions, cords or terminal branches.
