Neuroanatomy · Beginner

Neuroanatomy foundations: organisation, planes and terminology

18 min studyFree learning resourceIncludes memory aid

Lesson overview

Neuroanatomy studies nervous system structures and their connections. The CNS comprises brain and spinal cord; the PNS includes cranial and spinal nerves, ganglia and receptors. Grey matter contains neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, synapses and glia; white matter is rich in myelinated axons. Collections of cell bodies are called nuclei in the CNS and usually ganglia in the PNS. A tract is an axon bundle in the CNS; a nerve is a bundle in the PNS. Directional terms include rostral, caudal, dorsal and ventral, but the neuraxis bends between brain and spinal cord.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish CNS and PNS.
  • Use tract, nerve, nucleus, ganglion and cortex accurately.
  • Explain the bend of the neuraxis.

High-yield points

  • Ascending pathways generally carry sensory information toward the brain; descending pathways carry motor commands away from the brain.
  • In the cerebrum, dorsal is generally superior and ventral inferior; in the spinal cord, dorsal is posterior and ventral anterior.
  • Neural localisation combines symptoms, examination and pathway anatomy.
  • A single symptom rarely identifies a precise lesion by itself.

Memory aid / mnemonic

“Nucleus inside, ganglion outside; tract inside, nerve outside.” CNS = brain + cord; PNS = nerves + ganglia.

Study tip: hide this section, explain the concept aloud, then check whether you remembered the sequence and its rationale.

Clinical connection

Sudden weakness, facial droop, speech disturbance, seizure or new severe neurological symptoms require urgent medical assessment.

Check your understanding

  1. What does “ipsilateral” mean?
    Reveal answer

    On the same side of the body as the reference structure or lesion.

  2. What is the neuraxis?
    Reveal answer

    The conceptual axis of the developing and adult nervous system; its bend explains why directional terms differ between the forebrain and spinal cord.

  3. How does a tract differ from a peripheral nerve?
    Reveal answer

    A tract is a CNS axon bundle; a nerve is a PNS axon bundle.

Orient yourself before memorising structures

The nervous system is divided structurally into the central nervous system (CNS)—brain and spinal cord—and the peripheral nervous system (PNS)—nerves, ganglia and peripheral receptors. Functionally, sensory (afferent) pathways carry information toward the CNS; motor (efferent) pathways carry commands away. The somatic motor system supplies skeletal muscle, while autonomic pathways regulate smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands.

Grey matter and white matter

Grey matter contains neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, synapses and glia; white matter is rich in axons, many of them myelinated. In the cerebrum, much grey matter forms the superficial cortex and deeper nuclei. In the spinal cord, grey matter is central and white matter surrounds it. These are useful patterns, not universal rules for every structure.

Names for collections of nervous tissue

  • Nucleus: a collection of neuronal cell bodies within the CNS.
  • Ganglion: a collection of neuronal cell bodies in the PNS.
  • Tract: a bundle of axons within the CNS.
  • Nerve: a bundle of axons in the PNS.
  • Cortex: a sheet or layer of grey matter at the surface of an organ, especially cerebrum or cerebellum.
  • Commissure: axons connecting corresponding or related regions across the midline.

Directional language and the bent neuraxis

In the spinal cord, dorsal generally means posterior and ventral means anterior. In the forebrain, dorsal is generally superior and ventral inferior. The neuraxis bends during development, so a single set of compass terms cannot be applied mechanically to every level. Rostral means toward the nose/front of the brain and caudal toward the tail/back, with context needed around the flexures.

For any section, first identify whether it is a transverse, sagittal or coronal section, then identify orientation markers and the level. Only then interpret the position of nuclei, tracts or ventricles.

Clinical localisation: structure before diagnosis

A lesion may affect a nucleus, a long tract, a peripheral nerve, a neuromuscular junction or muscle. To localise, combine the pattern of weakness or sensory loss with reflexes, cranial nerve findings, coordination and time course. For example, a spinal cord lesion may produce findings below a level, while a peripheral nerve lesion follows that nerve’s territory. Real localisation requires a complete clinical assessment; one symptom rarely proves one precise site.

Quick self-test

  1. What is the difference between a tract and a nerve?
  2. Where is spinal cord grey matter relative to white matter?
  3. Why can dorsal mean different directions in brain and spinal cord?
Reveal answers and explanations

1. Tracts are CNS axon bundles; nerves are PNS axon bundles. 2. Grey matter is central, surrounded by white matter. 3. The neuraxis bends during development, changing orientation across levels.

Deep framework: how to build a neuroanatomical localisation

Start with the level

Before naming a disease, decide whether the pattern best fits brain, brainstem, spinal cord, nerve root, plexus, peripheral nerve, neuromuscular junction or muscle. A cerebral hemispheric lesion may combine contralateral face/limb findings with language, neglect, visual-field or cognitive changes. Brainstem lesions may produce “crossed” findings—cranial nerve signs on the side of the lesion with long-tract body signs on the opposite side. Spinal cord lesions can affect long tracts and segmental structures below a level. Root lesions often produce dermatomal sensory symptoms, myotomal weakness and reduced segmental reflexes. Peripheral nerve lesions follow that nerve’s territory rather than a whole dermatome.

Upper versus lower motor neuron pattern

Upper motor neuron pathways descend from cortex and brainstem to influence spinal and cranial motor circuits. A lesion above the anterior horn cell or cranial motor nucleus may cause weakness, increased tone and brisk reflexes after the acute phase, sometimes with an extensor plantar response. Lower motor neuron injury affects anterior horn cells, motor roots, plexuses, peripheral motor nerves or motor cranial nerve nuclei/axons and may cause flaccid weakness, reduced reflexes, wasting and fasciculations. Acute severe central lesions can initially produce reduced tone/reflexes, so timing matters.

Grey matter, white matter and connectivity

Grey matter contains neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, synapses and local circuitry; white matter consists mainly of myelinated axons organised into tracts. A nucleus is a CNS collection of neuronal cell bodies; a ganglion is a PNS collection. A tract is a bundle of CNS axons; a nerve is a bundle of PNS axons. A decussation is a crossing of fibres; a commissure connects the two sides. These terms describe location and connectivity, not simply appearance.

Use a five-question localisation sequence

  1. Which functions are abnormal: motor, sensory, coordination, cranial nerve, autonomic, language or cognition?
  2. Is the pattern unilateral, bilateral, segmental, dermatomal or nerve-territory based?
  3. Are face and body findings on the same side or opposite sides?
  4. Which pathway decussates, and at what level?
  5. Which single anatomical site explains the largest number of findings with the fewest assumptions?

This is a learning framework, not a substitute for a full neurological examination or urgent assessment of new deficits.

Case-based practice

  1. Weakness of the right arm and leg with aphasia suggests which broad site?
    Reveal reasoning

    A left cerebral hemispheric lesion involving dominant-hemisphere language and motor networks is a leading localisation. Sudden onset is an emergency.

  2. Left facial weakness from a cranial nerve nucleus lesion plus right-sided body weakness suggests what broad region?
    Reveal answer

    The left brainstem is a classic consideration because cranial nerve nuclear/fascicular findings may be ipsilateral while long-tract body findings are contralateral.

  3. Why is “numbness” alone insufficient to localise a lesion?
    Reveal answer

    One must clarify modality, distribution, onset, associated weakness/reflex changes and whether findings follow a nerve, root, spinal level, sensory pathway or cortical pattern.
