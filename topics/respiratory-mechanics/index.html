Respiratory mechanics, lung volumes and gas exchange

Ventilation moves air between atmosphere and alveoli; external respiration exchanges gases between alveoli and pulmonary capillary blood; internal respiration exchanges gases between systemic blood and tissues. During quiet inspiration, diaphragm contraction increases thoracic volume and lowers alveolar pressure below atmospheric pressure, drawing air into the lungs. Quiet expiration is usually passive as respiratory muscles relax and elastic recoil reduces lung volume. Compliance describes the change in volume produced by a change in pressure. Elastic recoil opposes expansion. Surfactant, produced by type II alveolar cells, reduces surface tension and helps stabilise alveoli. Airway resistance depends strongly on airway radius, smooth muscle tone, mucus and lung volume. Pulmonary ventilation is not identical to alveolar ventilation because some inspired air remains in conducting airways as anatomical dead space. Oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse down partial-pressure gradients across the alveolar-capillary membrane. Oxygen is transported mainly bound to haemoglobin; carbon dioxide is carried mainly as bicarbonate, with additional dissolved and protein-bound forms. Ventilation-perfusion matching is important: well-ventilated alveoli need adequate perfusion and vice versa for efficient gas exchange.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish ventilation, external respiration and internal respiration.
  • Explain quiet inspiration and expiration.
  • Describe compliance, surfactant, dead space and gas transport.

High-yield study points

  • Inspiration increases thoracic volume and lowers alveolar pressure relative to atmosphere.
  • Quiet expiration is mainly passive due to elastic recoil.
  • Compliance describes volume change per pressure change; surfactant reduces alveolar surface tension.
  • Alveolar ventilation excludes air that remains in conducting dead space.
  • Oxygen is carried mainly by haemoglobin; most carbon dioxide is transported as bicarbonate.
  • Gas exchange depends on diffusion gradients and ventilation-perfusion matching.

Clinical connection

Obstructive and restrictive lung disorders affect airflow, lung expansion or both in different ways. Spirometry and other tests are interpreted with symptoms, examination and appropriate clinical assessment.

Check your understanding

  1. Which muscle is the principal driver of quiet inspiration?
    Reveal answer

    The diaphragm.

  2. What happens to intrapleural pressure during quiet inspiration?
    Reveal answer

    It becomes more negative as the thoracic cavity expands, helping expand the lungs.

  3. What does lung compliance describe?
    Reveal answer

    The change in lung volume for a given change in transpulmonary pressure; it reflects distensibility.

Visual orientation: lungs and thoracic mechanics

During quiet inspiration, diaphragm contraction increases thoracic volume and intrapleural pressure becomes more negative, allowing alveolar pressure to fall slightly below atmospheric pressure so air flows inward. During quiet expiration, inspiratory muscles relax and elastic recoil raises alveolar pressure above atmospheric pressure, driving airflow outward. Forced breathing recruits additional muscles and changes these pressure relationships.
