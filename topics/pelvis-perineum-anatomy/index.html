Pelvic cavity, pelvic floor and perineum

The bony pelvis is formed by the hip bones, sacrum and coccyx. It transfers trunk weight to the lower limbs and protects pelvic viscera. The pelvic cavity contains the urinary bladder, distal ureters, rectum and internal reproductive organs, with arrangements that differ by sex and individual variation. The pelvic floor, especially the levator ani complex with coccygeus and associated fascia, supports pelvic organs and contributes to continence. The perineum lies inferior to the pelvic diaphragm and is divided into urogenital and anal triangles. Pudendal nerve branches and internal pudendal vessels are key structures in the perineum.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the bony pelvis and pelvic floor.
  • Distinguish pelvic cavity from perineum.
  • Explain the broad roles of the pudendal nerve and pelvic floor.

High-yield study points

  • The pelvic inlet separates greater and lesser pelvis; the pelvic outlet is bounded by bony and ligamentous landmarks.
  • The levator ani helps support pelvic viscera and resist increases in intra-abdominal pressure.
  • The perineal body is a central fibromuscular point for several pelvic-floor and perineal muscles.
  • The pudendal nerve carries important somatic sensory and motor fibres to the perineum.
  • Pelvic organs are related to peritoneum differently; not all pelvic organs are fully covered by it.

Clinical connection

Pelvic-floor dysfunction can contribute to urinary or faecal incontinence and pelvic organ prolapse. Pelvic pain and masses have multiple possible causes and require appropriate evaluation.

  2. What are the two major triangles of the perineum?
    Reveal answer

    The urogenital triangle anteriorly and the anal triangle posteriorly.

  3. Why is the pudendal nerve important in perineal anatomy?
    Reveal answer

    It provides major somatic sensory and motor innervation to the perineum, including the external anal and external urethral sphincter regions.

Check your understanding: pelvis and perineum

  1. What is the broad boundary between the greater and lesser pelvis?
    Answer and explanation

    The pelvic brim (pelvic inlet), formed by the sacral promontory, arcuate lines, pectineal lines and pubic crest/symphysis region.

  2. What is the central functional role of the pelvic diaphragm?
    Answer and explanation

    It supports pelvic viscera, contributes to continence and participates in pressure management and pelvic organ support. It is formed mainly by levator ani and coccygeus with associated fascia.

  3. Why is the perineum divided into urogenital and anal triangles?
    Answer and explanation

    The line between the ischial tuberosities divides the diamond-shaped perineum into anterior urogenital and posterior anal triangles, organising the anatomy of their contents.

Pelvic walls, boundaries and compartments

The pelvic cavity is a three-dimensional, curved space rather than a simple container. Its anterior wall is formed mainly by the pubic bodies and symphysis; the posterior wall includes the sacrum, coccyx and piriformis muscles; the lateral walls include the hip bones, obturator internus muscles and their fascia. The pelvic diaphragm forms the principal muscular floor. The pelvic brim marks the transition between greater and lesser pelvis, while the pelvic outlet is closed dynamically by muscle, fascia and perineal structures.

The obturator internus covers much of the lateral pelvic wall and its tendon turns through the lesser sciatic foramen toward the greater trochanter. Piriformis arises from the anterior sacrum and exits through the greater sciatic foramen; it divides the passage into supra- and infrapiriform spaces for important neurovascular structures. These muscle relationships help orient the sacral plexus and gluteal vessels during dissection and imaging.

Pelvic fascia and organ support

Endopelvic fascia is connective tissue around pelvic organs, vessels and nerves; named ligamentous condensations such as the uterosacral and cardinal (transverse cervical) ligaments are regional thickenings rather than isolated cords with identical boundaries in every person. The puboprostatic/pubovesical and rectal support tissues also contribute to organ position. Support is shared between fascia, connective tissue, pelvic-floor muscle and the geometry of the organs themselves. A prolapse cannot be explained by one ligament alone.

Male and female pelvic relations

In the typical female pelvis, the bladder lies anterior to the uterus and vagina, while the rectum lies posteriorly. The cervix is closely related to the ureters and uterine vessels; the ovaries lie on the lateral pelvic walls but their position can vary. In the typical male pelvis, the prostate lies below the bladder, the seminal vesicles lie posterior to the bladder, and the rectum lies behind these structures. In both sexes, the ureter, internal iliac vessels, autonomic plexuses and pelvic floor have important relations to the viscera. These are general maps, not guarantees of exact position in every individual.

Neural control of pelvic function

Pelvic organs receive autonomic and somatic input. Parasympathetic pelvic splanchnic nerves from S2–S4 contribute to detrusor contraction and rectal motility, while sympathetic fibres reach the pelvis through hypogastric pathways. Somatic control through sacral roots and pudendal branches is important for external sphincters and perineal sensation. Normal voiding and defaecation require coordination between peripheral nerves, spinal circuits, higher centres, smooth muscle, striated muscle and sensory feedback.

How to study pelvic anatomy in the laboratory

  1. Orient the specimen using sacrum, pubic symphysis, ischial spines and obturator foramina before naming soft tissues.
  2. Identify pelvic wall muscles and the greater/lesser sciatic foramina before tracing nerves and vessels.
  3. Locate the bladder, rectum and reproductive organs, then map the peritoneal reflections and subperitoneal spaces.
  4. Trace the internal iliac branches and sacral plexus as systems, not as isolated lists.
  5. Relate the pelvic diaphragm to the perineal membrane and superficial perineal muscles; they occupy different levels.
  6. Use photographs as orientation aids, but confirm structures with a labelled atlas and supervised practical teaching.

Core spatial framework: walls, cavity and outlet

Study the pelvis as a continuous ring and a three-dimensional basin rather than as a flat set of landmarks. The bony ring comprises the paired hip bones and sacrum, with the coccyx contributing posteriorly. The pelvic brim marks the transition between greater and lesser pelvis. The lesser pelvis contains the pelvic viscera and is bounded laterally by the hip bones and obturator internus, posteriorly by the sacrum and coccyx with piriformis overlying the sacral plexus, and inferiorly by the pelvic diaphragm. The perineum lies below that diaphragm. This distinction is essential: the pelvic cavity is not synonymous with the perineum, and pelvic-floor muscles form a boundary between them.

The pelvic inlet is traced from the sacral promontory along the arcuate line, pecten pubis and pubic crest to the superior border of the symphysis. The outlet is irregular and includes the pubic arch anteriorly, ischial tuberosities laterally, sacrotuberous ligaments posterolaterally and coccyx posteriorly. The inlet, midpelvis and outlet have different dimensions; no single diameter describes the entire birth canal or predicts an individual labour outcome.

How to learn the relationships

  1. Identify bone first: promontory, sacroiliac joints, ischial spine, ischial tuberosity, pubic symphysis and coccyx.
  2. Add the wall muscles: obturator internus laterally and piriformis posteriorly.
  3. Place the pelvic diaphragm across the outlet, noting the hiatuses and the relation of levator ani to the viscera.
  4. Place the bladder and urethra anteriorly, reproductive organs centrally where present, and rectum posteriorly; then add fascia, vessels and nerves.
  5. Finally, trace the perineum inferiorly and identify its urogenital and anal triangles.

This layered method is more reliable than memorising isolated lists because it supports interpretation of surgical approaches, pelvic organ prolapse, nerve injury, haemorrhage and cross-sectional scans.

Pelvic anatomy in depth: a three-dimensional guide

Orientation and the pelvic brim

Begin by orienting the pelvis as it lies in anatomical position: the anterior superior iliac spines and pubic tubercles are approximately in the same vertical plane, while the sacrum curves posteriorly. The pelvic brim forms an oblique boundary between the greater pelvis, which is continuous with the lower abdomen, and the lesser pelvis, which encloses the pelvic cavity. Its landmarks run from the sacral promontory along the ala of the sacrum, arcuate line of the ilium, pectineal line and pubic crest to the superior border of the pubic symphysis. The inlet is therefore not a horizontal circle; it is an oblique oval whose dimensions and shape vary among individuals.

The pelvic cavity is bounded anteriorly by the pubic bodies and symphysis; posteriorly by the sacrum and coccyx; laterally by the hip bones, obturator internus and fascia; and inferiorly by the pelvic diaphragm. The obturator internus lines the lateral wall and its tendon turns through the lesser sciatic foramen. Piriformis arises from the anterior sacrum and exits through the greater sciatic foramen, creating important supra- and infrapiriform routes for nerves and vessels. These muscular landmarks help orient the sacral plexus, gluteal vessels and pelvic sidewall during dissection and cross-sectional imaging.

Pelvic floor as a dynamic support system

The pelvic diaphragm is formed principally by levator ani and coccygeus with their fascia. It supports viscera while permitting urethral, vaginal and anal passage. Puborectalis loops around the anorectal junction and contributes to the anorectal angle; pubococcygeus and iliococcygeus provide additional support and closure. Pelvic-floor function depends on strength, endurance, timing, ability to relax, connective-tissue integrity and coordination with the diaphragm and abdominal wall. Continence is not simply a matter of keeping the muscles constantly contracted: normal voiding and defaecation require appropriately timed relaxation as well as contraction.

Fascial support and organ relationships

Pelvic viscera are supported by a combination of the pelvic floor, endopelvic connective tissue, ligamentous condensations, organ attachments and bony geometry. Terms such as cardinal, uterosacral and pubovesical/puboprostatic ligaments describe important regions of connective-tissue support, but their boundaries are not identical to isolated cords. The uterus, bladder and rectum move and change shape with filling, pregnancy, posture and age. The peritoneum covers only selected superior surfaces; much of the lower pelvis is subperitoneal or extraperitoneal. These distinctions matter when interpreting fluid, infection, tumour spread and surgical planes.

How to study a pelvic specimen

  1. Identify the pubic symphysis anteriorly, sacrum posteriorly and pelvic brim before naming soft tissues.
  2. Locate piriformis, obturator internus and the greater and lesser sciatic foramina; use these to orient the sacral plexus and pelvic vessels.
  3. Identify the bladder, rectum and reproductive organs, then trace peritoneal reflections rather than assuming every organ is intraperitoneal.
  4. Locate levator ani and coccygeus; distinguish the pelvic diaphragm from the more inferior perineal membrane and superficial perineal muscles.
  5. Trace the internal iliac vessels and sacral plexus along the lateral wall, then follow branches toward organs, gluteal region and perineum.

Applied perspective: pelvic fractures may injure organs or vessels; pelvic-floor injury can affect continence and organ support; ureteric relations are important in pelvic surgery; and changes in the perineum during childbirth can involve the perineal body or anal sphincter complex. These are anatomical explanations, not diagnostic rules. Clinical assessment and management require qualified professionals.

Three-dimensional comparison: male and female bony pelvis

The bony pelvis forms a ring that transmits body weight from the vertebral column to the lower limbs and protects pelvic viscera. The pelvic inlet separates the greater and lesser pelvis, while the outlet is bounded by the pubic arch anteriorly, ischial tuberosities laterally, and coccyx and sacrotuberous ligaments posteriorly. Shape varies substantially between individuals; sex-related averages should not be treated as absolute rules for any one person.

Clinical and functional interpretation

When studying a pelvic image, identify the sacral promontory, arcuate and pectineal lines, pubic symphysis, ischial spines and tuberosities, and the greater and lesser sciatic foramina. Then ask which structures pass through each opening. The greater sciatic foramen provides a route between pelvis and gluteal region; the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels leave the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, pass around the ischial spine/sacrospinous ligament region, and enter the perineum through the lesser sciatic foramen. This relationship explains why the ischial spine is an important landmark in pelvic examination and selected anaesthetic procedures.

The pelvic floor is not simply a rigid hammock. Levator ani and coccygeus, connective tissue supports, pelvic fascia, and the coordinated action of urethral and anal sphincter mechanisms work together with abdominal pressure and breathing. Continence depends on both anatomy and neuromuscular timing. A structural diagram can explain relationships, but it cannot determine an individual's pelvic-floor function or diagnose a disorder.

Applied reasoning prompt

A learner is asked to explain why a pudendal nerve lesion may affect perineal sensation and parts of voluntary continence. A strong answer should trace the nerve from the sacral plexus, through the greater sciatic foramen, around the ischial spine, and into the pudendal canal, then connect its inferior rectal, perineal and dorsal nerve of the clitoris/penis branches with their sensory and motor territories. Avoid saying that every continence problem is caused by pudendal nerve injury; continence is multifactorial.
