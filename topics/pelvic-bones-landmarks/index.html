Bony pelvis: bones, joints, inlet, outlet and measurements

A detailed study of the pelvic girdle, pelvic brim, inlet and outlet, sex-related variation, ligaments and applied bony landmarks.

Return to pelvis & perineum courseBrowse all topics

1. Formation and functions of the bony pelvis

The bony pelvis consists of the right and left hip bones, sacrum and coccyx. Each hip bone develops from the ilium, ischium and pubis, which meet at the acetabulum during growth. The acetabulum receives the head of the femur; the obturator foramen is largely closed by the obturator membrane, leaving a canal for obturator neurovascular structures. The pelvis transfers body weight from the vertebral column to the lower limbs, provides attachment for trunk and lower-limb muscles, protects pelvic viscera and forms the bony framework of the birth canal.

The sacroiliac joints transmit load between sacrum and ilia. Their stability depends on interlocking articular surfaces and strong anterior, interosseous and posterior sacroiliac ligaments. The pubic symphysis is a secondary cartilaginous joint containing a fibrocartilaginous disc; small movement can occur, and ligamentous laxity may change during pregnancy. The sacrococcygeal joint permits limited movement and varies between individuals.

2. Greater and lesser pelvis

The pelvic brim separates the greater (false) pelvis above from the lesser (true) pelvis below. The greater pelvis supports lower abdominal viscera and is continuous with the abdominal cavity. The lesser pelvis contains much of the urinary bladder, rectum and internal reproductive organs and is bounded by the pelvic walls and floor. The distinction is topographical, not a complete partition between abdominal and pelvic organs.

Pelvic inlet

The inlet is outlined posteriorly by the sacral promontory and alae, laterally by the arcuate lines of the ilia and pectineal lines of the pubes, and anteriorly by the pubic crest and superior border of the symphysis. Obstetric descriptions include the anteroposterior, transverse and oblique dimensions; their clinical interpretation requires formal assessment rather than judging labour outcome from one measurement.

Pelvic cavity and outlet

The cavity curves from the inlet toward the outlet. The outlet is bounded anteriorly by the inferior border of the pubic symphysis, anterolaterally by the ischiopubic rami, laterally by the ischial tuberosities, posterolaterally by the sacrotuberous ligaments and posteriorly by the coccyx. The coccyx can move posteriorly to a variable degree. The pelvic floor spans the outlet and is not simply a flat muscular sheet.

3. Sex-related variation and individual anatomy

On average, female pelvic morphology tends to have a wider subpubic angle, a broader and less projecting sacrum, and a more rounded or transversely oval inlet than the average male pelvis. Male pelvic morphology tends to be narrower and deeper, with a more acute subpubic angle. These are population-level tendencies, not reliable rules for identifying an individual’s sex: there is substantial overlap, and age, ancestry, body size, pathology and individual variation matter. Avoid treating one feature as diagnostic.

4. Ligaments, foramina and palpable landmarks

The sacrospinous ligament runs from the sacrum and coccyx to the ischial spine; the sacrotuberous ligament extends toward the ischial tuberosity. Together with the greater and lesser sciatic notches, they form the greater and lesser sciatic foramina. The piriformis passes through the greater sciatic foramen; the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels leave the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, pass around the ischial spine/sacrospinous ligament region, and enter the perineum through the lesser sciatic foramen. This route is fundamental to understanding pudendal nerve block and entrapment anatomy.

The pubic symphysis, iliac crest, anterior superior iliac spine, ischial tuberosity and sacral landmarks help orient surface examination, positioning and radiological interpretation. Deep pelvic structures are not all palpable and should not be assessed by forceful examination.

Applied anatomy

  • Pelvic-ring fractures may be associated with major haemorrhage and injuries to bladder, urethra, bowel, nerves or vessels; suspected major trauma requires emergency care.
  • Sacroiliac-region pain has several possible causes and cannot be diagnosed from a single landmark.
  • Pelvic inlet/outlet morphology is relevant to obstetric anatomy, but clinical decisions depend on the full maternal and fetal context.

Self-test

  1. Which structures form the pelvic brim?
    Answer

    Sacral promontory and alae, arcuate lines, pectineal lines, pubic crest and superior symphyseal border.

  2. How do the sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments contribute to pelvic anatomy?
    Answer

    They convert the sciatic notches into greater and lesser sciatic foramina and provide important attachment/stability.

  3. Why should sex-related pelvic features be described as tendencies?
    Answer

    There is substantial individual and population overlap; one feature cannot reliably classify every pelvis.

Study and safety note

This is a learning resource for anatomy study, not a substitute for supervised dissection, clinical training or professional medical judgement. Anatomical patterns vary; clinical symptoms must be interpreted by qualified professionals.

Advanced osteology: pelvic ring, load transfer and ligament mechanics

The pelvis is both a locomotor load-transfer structure and a protective ring for the pelvic viscera. During standing, trunk load passes from the lumbar spine to the sacral base, through the sacroiliac joints into the ilia, then toward the acetabula and lower limbs. The sacroiliac joints have irregular, interlocking surfaces and limited movement; their stability depends strongly on the interosseous sacroiliac ligament and supporting posterior and anterior ligaments. The sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments convert the greater and lesser sciatic notches into foramina and resist excessive sacral rotation. The iliolumbar ligament helps stabilise the lumbosacral junction.

Each hip bone develops by fusion of the ilium, ischium and pubis at the acetabulum. The triradiate cartilage is important during growth. The acetabular lunate surface bears load, while the acetabular labrum deepens the socket and improves joint congruence. The obturator foramen is mostly closed by the obturator membrane; the obturator canal provides a passage for the obturator nerve and vessels. The ischial spine is a particularly useful landmark for the pudendal nerve, sacrospinous ligament and obstetric assessment.

Pelvic inlet, cavity and outlet: boundaries and dimensions

The inlet is bounded posteriorly by the sacral promontory and alae, laterally by the arcuate line and pecten pubis, and anteriorly by the pubic crest and superior margin of the symphysis. Its anteroposterior, transverse and oblique dimensions describe different axes and should not be confused with the midpelvic interspinous diameter. The midpelvis is often clinically discussed around the ischial spines; the outlet is bounded anteriorly by the pubic arch, laterally by the ischial tuberosities and sacrotuberous ligaments, and posteriorly by the coccyx. Pelvic outlet dimensions change with coccygeal mobility and position.

In obstetrics, the anatomical conjugate extends from the sacral promontory to the superior border of the pubic symphysis; the obstetric conjugate is the shortest anteroposterior inlet diameter and is estimated indirectly rather than measured directly by routine digital examination. The diagonal conjugate can be assessed clinically by trained practitioners and used as an estimate, but it is not a stand-alone predictor of labour. The functional birth canal is influenced by soft tissues, fetal size and position, pelvic mobility, uterine forces and clinical circumstances.

Sex-related variation without oversimplification

On average, female pelves tend to have a wider subpubic angle, a broader and less projecting sacrum, and a more rounded or transversely oval inlet than male pelves. Male pelves on average tend to be heavier, with a narrower subpubic angle and more prominent ischial spines. These are population-level tendencies with substantial overlap; no single feature can determine sex reliably in every individual, and pelvic morphology is influenced by growth, age, ancestry, body size and individual variation.

Palpable landmarks and imaging

Locate the anterior superior iliac spines, iliac crests, pubic symphysis, ischial tuberosities and sacral hiatus on the living body where appropriate. On radiographs and CT, compare both sides and assess cortical continuity, joint alignment and the sacral foramina. On MRI, marrow signal, cartilage and soft-tissue attachments are more readily assessed. Pelvic fractures may disrupt the ring at more than one point; high-energy trauma requires systematic evaluation because bleeding and associated visceral injury may be life-threatening.

Common examination traps

  • The pelvic brim separates greater and lesser pelvis; it is not the pelvic floor.
  • The ischial spine is a key pudendal-nerve landmark; the ischial tuberosity is the major sitting-bone landmark.
  • The pelvic outlet is not a simple circle; its posterior boundary includes the coccyx and sacrotuberous ligaments.
  • Pelvimetry describes anatomy but cannot independently determine safe mode of delivery.

Advanced pelvic osteology, mechanics and measurement

Pelvic inlet, cavity and outlet as a continuous canal

The inlet is bounded by the sacral promontory, sacral alae, arcuate lines, pectineal lines, pubic crests and superior pubic symphysis. The midpelvis is shaped by the sacrum, ischial spines and pelvic sidewalls; the outlet is bounded by the inferior pubic arch anteriorly, ischial tuberosities laterally, and coccyx and sacrotuberous ligaments posteriorly. These boundaries form a curved canal rather than a straight tube. The fetal head and other structures descend through changing planes, so inlet dimensions alone do not describe the entire pelvis.

Important diameters and their meaning

The anteroposterior inlet diameter is described by conjugates. The anatomical (true) conjugate extends from the sacral promontory to the superior border of the pubic symphysis; the obstetric conjugate is the shortest anteroposterior distance between the promontory and the posterior surface of the symphysis; the diagonal conjugate is measured clinically from the promontory to the inferior border of the symphysis and can be used to estimate the obstetric conjugate. These are not interchangeable. Interspinous distance at the midpelvis and transverse dimensions also matter. Values vary with measurement method, population and individual anatomy; no single measurement predicts labour outcome by itself.

Joints, ligaments and load transfer

The sacroiliac joints combine irregular articular surfaces with strong anterior, interosseous and posterior ligaments. Their small movements are important for load transfer between the vertebral column and lower limbs. The sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments convert the sciatic notches into greater and lesser sciatic foramina. The pubic symphysis is a secondary cartilaginous joint with a fibrocartilaginous disc; limited movement occurs and ligamentous laxity can change during pregnancy. Pelvic stability reflects the shape of the bones, ligament tension, muscle forces and coordinated trunk and hip control.

Sex-related variation and individual diversity

Average pelvic differences are associated with reproductive anatomy and development, but there is substantial overlap between individuals. Pelvic shape should not be reduced to a rigid binary checklist or used to infer an individual's identity. In obstetric contexts, pelvic anatomy is only one factor among fetal size and position, soft-tissue compliance, maternal health and labour progress. Clinical decisions must be based on trained assessment rather than a textbook diagram.

Practical osteology checklist

  • Identify the iliac crest, anterior and posterior superior iliac spines, acetabulum, obturator foramen, ischial spine and ischial tuberosity.
  • Trace the pelvic brim continuously from the sacral promontory to the pubic symphysis.
  • Distinguish the greater sciatic foramen from the lesser sciatic foramen using the sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments.
  • Relate the acetabulum to the femoral head and the sacroiliac joints to axial load transfer.
  • On radiographs or CT, assess symmetry and fractures while remembering that projection and patient positioning alter apparent shape.
