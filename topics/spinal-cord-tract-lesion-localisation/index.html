Spinal cord pathways and lesion localisation

24 min studyFree learning resourceIncludes memory aid

Lesson overview

The spinal cord contains segmental grey matter surrounded by white matter columns. Dorsal roots carry sensory afferents into the cord; ventral roots carry motor efferents out. Dorsal columns carry vibration, discriminative touch and conscious proprioception; the anterolateral system carries pain and temperature; corticospinal tracts support voluntary movement. Because these pathways cross at different levels, a unilateral cord lesion may cause ipsilateral motor and dorsal-column deficits with contralateral pain-temperature loss below the lesion. Segmental findings, dermatomes, reflexes and autonomic function refine localisation.

Learning objectives

  • Identify dorsal and ventral roots and major tracts.
  • Explain how crossing levels determine deficit side.
  • Apply a structured approach to spinal cord lesion patterns.

High-yield points

  • Dorsal root ganglia contain primary sensory neuron cell bodies; ventral horn motor neurons send axons through ventral roots.
  • Lateral corticospinal lesions in the cord typically affect ipsilateral voluntary motor control below the lesion.
  • Dorsal column deficits are ipsilateral below a spinal lesion; spinothalamic deficits are often contralateral and begin slightly below it.
  • Brown-Séquard syndrome is a teaching pattern; real cord injuries may be incomplete or mixed.

Memory aid / mnemonic

Dorsal detects; ventral drives. Hemicord cue: ipsilateral motor + vibration/proprioception loss; contralateral pain/temperature loss below the lesion.

Study tip: hide this section, explain the concept aloud, then check whether you remembered the sequence and its rationale.

Clinical connection

New weakness, loss of sensation, saddle anaesthesia or bladder/bowel dysfunction—especially after trauma—requires urgent medical assessment.
