Physiotherapy: foundations, scope and professional role

An introduction to physiotherapy, movement, function, rehabilitation and person-centred practice.

What is physiotherapy?

Physiotherapy is a health profession concerned with human movement and function across the lifespan. Physiotherapists assess movement-related impairments and activity limitations, identify factors that affect participation, and work with people to prevent disability, improve capacity and support recovery. Care may include education, exercise, movement training, manual techniques when indicated, assistive devices, and advice about self-management.

Physiotherapy is not simply massage or the treatment of sports injuries. It spans musculoskeletal, neurological, cardiorespiratory, paediatric, geriatric, community and rehabilitation practice. The specific scope of practice depends on professional training, local regulation and the clinical setting.

Core concepts

  • Movement: how joints, muscles, the nervous system and sensory systems cooperate to produce motion.
  • Function: the ability to perform activities such as walking, dressing, lifting, breathing effectively or returning to work.
  • Participation: involvement in family, school, work and community life.
  • Person-centred care: decisions are shared with the person and reflect their goals, preferences, environment and resources.
  • Evidence-informed practice: clinical decisions combine the best available research, professional expertise and the individual's values and circumstances.

Where physiotherapists work

Practice settings include hospitals, outpatient clinics, rehabilitation centres, sports environments, primary care, schools, community services, workplaces and home-based care. A physiotherapist may help a person recover walking after stroke, regain shoulder movement after injury, improve exercise tolerance after respiratory illness, or maintain mobility during ageing.

A typical care pathway

  1. Establish the person's concerns, history, goals and relevant precautions.
  2. Assess impairments and functional activities using appropriate measures.
  3. Agree on priorities and a plan with the person.
  4. Deliver and adapt interventions while monitoring response and safety.
  5. Reassess outcomes, support self-management and plan follow-up or referral.

Clinical connection

A patient with knee pain may need more than a local examination. Walking pattern, strength, joint movement, work demands, confidence, other health conditions and personal goals may all influence the plan. Persistent red flags or findings outside the physiotherapist's scope require appropriate medical assessment or referral.

Further study

Use this lesson as a foundation and consult current professional guidance, validated assessment resources and relevant research for clinical decisions. Clinical practice must follow local professional standards and individual patient needs.

Check your understanding

  1. What is the main goal of a physiotherapy assessment?
    Reveal answer

    To understand the person's impairments, activity limitations, participation restrictions, context and goals so that a safe, individualised plan can be developed.

  2. Why is informed consent important before examination or treatment?
    Reveal answer

    It supports autonomy and requires clear explanation of the proposed procedure, likely benefits, risks and alternatives before the person agrees.

  3. Why should treatment goals be measurable and meaningful to the patient?
    Reveal answer

    They guide shared decisions, make progress easier to evaluate and keep treatment relevant to function and participation.

Learning objectives

  • Define physiotherapy in relation to movement, function, activity and participation.
  • Distinguish impairment-level goals from functional and participation goals.
  • Explain the roles of assessment, shared decision-making, intervention, reassessment and prevention.
  • Recognise professional responsibilities including consent, evidence-informed practice, scope of practice and referral.

From impairment to participation

Reduced ankle range of motion is an impairment; difficulty walking to work is an activity limitation; inability to fulfil a valued work or family role may be a participation restriction. Effective rehabilitation considers all these levels alongside the person's environment, preferences and resources.

From impairment to meaningful function

Physiotherapy assessment should connect body structures and functions to what a person can do and the roles they need or want to perform. The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) provides a useful framework: health condition, body functions and structures, activities, participation, environmental factors and personal factors influence one another.

  • Impairment: a problem in body function or structure, such as reduced ankle range, weakness or impaired balance.
  • Activity limitation: difficulty carrying out a task, such as walking, dressing, transferring or climbing stairs.
  • Participation restriction: difficulty being involved in life situations, such as returning to work, school, caregiving or community activities.
  • Contextual factors: home layout, transport, assistive devices, support, beliefs, fatigue, finances and access to services can influence outcomes.

Evidence-informed clinical reasoning

Good practice integrates the best available research, clinical expertise and the person's goals, preferences and circumstances. It does not mean applying one protocol identically to everyone. Choose an assessment measure because it answers a clinical question; document the baseline; agree a measurable goal; select an intervention with a clear rationale; monitor response; and modify the plan when progress, symptoms or priorities change.

Goals and outcome measures

A useful goal specifies the task or function, the expected level of performance and a time frame where appropriate. Use outcome measures that are reliable, valid, responsive to change and feasible for the person and setting. A change in a score should be interpreted alongside the person's experience and whether the change is meaningful in daily life.

Professional safety

Obtain informed consent, protect confidentiality, work within scope of practice and document clearly. Sudden neurological symptoms, severe chest pain, unexplained syncope, suspected fracture, signs of deep-vein thrombosis or rapidly worsening breathlessness require prompt medical assessment according to local pathways rather than routine progression of exercise.
