Abdominal wall, inguinal canal and hernias

The anterolateral abdominal wall includes skin, superficial fascia, three flat muscle layers—external oblique, internal oblique and transversus abdominis—and the rectus abdominis within the rectus sheath. These layers support viscera, contribute to trunk movement and raise intra-abdominal pressure. The inguinal canal is an oblique passage through the lower abdominal wall; in males it transmits the spermatic cord and in females the round ligament of the uterus, as well as the ilioinguinal nerve for part of its course. The deep and superficial inguinal rings and the relationship to the inferior epigastric vessels help distinguish common hernia pathways.

Learning objectives

  • Identify the major abdominal-wall muscle layers.
  • Describe the inguinal canal and its principal contents.
  • Explain the basic anatomical distinction between direct and indirect inguinal hernias.

High-yield study points

  • The external oblique fibres generally run inferomedially; internal oblique fibres generally run superomedially; transversus fibres are predominantly transverse.
  • The rectus sheath arrangement changes above and below the arcuate line.
  • Indirect inguinal hernias enter the deep ring and travel through the canal lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels; direct hernias protrude through a weakened posterior wall medial to those vessels.
  • The linea alba is a midline fibrous raphe; the linea semilunaris marks the lateral border of rectus abdominis.
  • The inguinal region is a natural area of weakness, but hernia risk reflects multiple anatomical and physiological factors.

Clinical connection

A painful, irreducible groin swelling, especially with vomiting or abdominal distension, can indicate an emergency and needs urgent medical assessment.
