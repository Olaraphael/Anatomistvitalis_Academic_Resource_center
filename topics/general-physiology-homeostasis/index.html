Physiology foundations: homeostasis, control systems and feedback

Physiology explains how living systems work, from ion movement across membranes to integrated organ-system responses. Homeostasis is the dynamic regulation of internal conditions within ranges compatible with life; it does not mean every variable remains perfectly constant. Many regulated variables are controlled through sensors, integrating centres and effectors that adjust the response to changing conditions.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the core concepts in this lesson.
  • Identify key structures, relationships or mechanisms.
  • Apply the concepts to a practical or clinical example.

High-yield study points

  • A regulated variable is monitored through receptors or sensors; information is integrated by a control centre or distributed network, and effectors change the variable.
  • Negative feedback opposes the initial deviation and is common in temperature regulation, blood glucose control and many endocrine loops.
  • Positive feedback amplifies a process until an endpoint, as in labour contractions or the amplification phase of blood clotting.
  • Feedforward control anticipates a demand, such as cardiovascular and respiratory adjustments at the start of exercise.
  • Set points and operating ranges can shift with circadian rhythms, hormones, fever, exercise and development.
  • Physiological systems are interdependent: ventilation affects blood gases and pH, kidneys regulate fluid and electrolytes, and hormones coordinate multiple organs.
  • Compensation may restore a variable partly without removing the underlying cause; chronic compensation can itself have costs.

Clinical connection

Understanding feedback helps distinguish a primary disturbance from the body's response. Fever, for example, involves a regulated change in hypothalamic set point, unlike many forms of uncontrolled hyperthermia.
