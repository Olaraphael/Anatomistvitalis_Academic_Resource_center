Physiology foundations: homeostasis, control systems and feedback

Physiology explains how living systems work, from ion movement across membranes to integrated organ-system responses. Homeostasis is the dynamic regulation of internal conditions within ranges compatible with life; it does not mean every variable remains perfectly constant. Many regulated variables are controlled through sensors, integrating centres and effectors that adjust the response to changing conditions.

Learning objectives

  • Define physiology and distinguish cellular, organ and systems physiology.
  • Explain homeostasis using regulated variables, sensors, control centres and effectors.
  • Compare negative feedback with positive feedback and give appropriate examples.
  • Relate disturbances in regulation to the development of physiological dysfunction.

High-yield study points

  • A regulated variable is monitored through receptors or sensors; information is integrated by a control centre or distributed network, and effectors change the variable.
  • Negative feedback opposes the initial deviation and is common in temperature regulation, blood glucose control and many endocrine loops.
  • Positive feedback amplifies a process until an endpoint, as in labour contractions or the amplification phase of blood clotting.
  • Feedforward control anticipates a demand, such as cardiovascular and respiratory adjustments at the start of exercise.
  • Set points and operating ranges can shift with circadian rhythms, hormones, fever, exercise and development.
  • Physiological systems are interdependent: ventilation affects blood gases and pH, kidneys regulate fluid and electrolytes, and hormones coordinate multiple organs.
  • Compensation may restore a variable partly without removing the underlying cause; chronic compensation can itself have costs.

Clinical connection

Understanding feedback helps distinguish a primary disturbance from the body's response. Fever, for example, involves a regulated change in hypothalamic set point, unlike many forms of uncontrolled hyperthermia.

Homeostasis as a control system

Homeostasis is dynamic regulation of the internal environment, not perfect constancy. A typical control loop includes a regulated variable, a sensor or receptor, an integrating centre or network, effectors and feedback. The response depends on the disturbance, the range of the variable and the capacity of the organ systems involved.

Negative feedback

Negative feedback opposes the initial deviation and stabilises a variable. If body temperature rises, thermoregulatory responses promote heat loss; as temperature approaches the regulated range, the response diminishes. Blood glucose regulation is another example: insulin and glucagon coordinate uptake, storage and release of fuels. Negative feedback does not mean a response is always small; it means the response counteracts the change in the regulated variable.

Positive feedback and feedforward

Positive feedback amplifies a process until an endpoint or limiting event. Oxytocin-associated uterine contractions during labour are a classic example: cervical stretch promotes oxytocin release, which strengthens contractions and increases stretch. Feedforward control anticipates a predictable demand; cardiovascular and respiratory activity rise around the start of exercise before all chemical changes have fully developed.

Set points, ranges and compensation

Many physiological variables operate within ranges rather than one rigid number. Circadian rhythms, exercise, age, hormones and illness can alter the operating range. Fever illustrates a regulated upward shift in hypothalamic temperature set point; heat stroke involves dangerous hyperthermia without the same set-point mechanism.

Compensation is the body's attempt to limit a disturbance. It may improve a measured variable while the underlying problem remains. For example, respiratory changes can compensate for a metabolic acid–base disturbance, but compensation does not necessarily remove the cause. Interpret laboratory results by considering the primary disturbance, expected compensation and clinical context.

Integration across organ systems

Ventilation and circulation coordinate oxygen delivery and carbon dioxide removal; kidneys regulate extracellular fluid, electrolytes and acid excretion; endocrine signals coordinate longer-term changes in metabolism, growth and reproduction; the nervous system supports rapid sensing and responses. No organ system maintains homeostasis in isolation.

Quick self-test

  1. What are the main components of a negative-feedback loop?
  2. How does positive feedback differ from negative feedback?
  3. Why does compensation not necessarily mean recovery?
Reveal answers and explanations

1. Sensor, integrating centre/network, effector and regulated variable with feedback. 2. Positive feedback amplifies the process; negative feedback counteracts the deviation. 3. Compensation may limit a disturbance without removing its cause.

Check your understanding

  1. What type of feedback is most commonly used to maintain a regulated variable near its operating range?
    Reveal answer

    Negative feedback, which opposes a change in the regulated variable.

  2. Name the basic parts of a homeostatic control system.
    Reveal answer

    A sensor/receptor, integrating or control centre, and effector.

  3. Is positive feedback always harmful?
    Reveal answer

    No. It amplifies a process in specific contexts, such as labour contractions, until a terminating event occurs.
