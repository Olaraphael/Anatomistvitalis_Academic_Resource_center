Descending motor pathways and upper versus lower motor neuron lesions

26 min studyFree learning resourceIncludes memory aid

Lesson overview

Voluntary movement depends on motor cortex, descending pathways, spinal and cranial motor circuits, basal ganglia, cerebellum and sensory feedback. Corticospinal axons pass through the internal capsule, cerebral peduncles, pons and medullary pyramids. Most cross at the pyramidal decussation to form the lateral corticospinal tract. Upper motor neurons influence lower motor neurons, which directly innervate skeletal muscle. Upper motor neuron lesions classically cause weakness, increased tone and exaggerated reflexes after the acute phase; lower motor neuron lesions cause weakness with reduced tone and reflexes and may produce atrophy and fasciculations.

Learning objectives

  • Trace the corticospinal pathway.
  • Distinguish upper and lower motor neuron signs.
  • Relate lesion level to side of weakness.

High-yield points

  • A lesion above the pyramidal crossing commonly causes contralateral weakness; a lateral spinal cord lesion affects ipsilateral descending fibres below it.
  • Upper motor neuron signs often include spasticity, hyperreflexia and extensor plantar response.
  • Lower motor neuron signs include flaccid weakness, hyporeflexia, atrophy and fasciculations.
  • Movement also depends on corticobulbar, cerebellar, basal ganglia and sensory systems.

Memory aid / mnemonic

UMN = often increased tone/reflexes; LMN = reduced tone/reflexes, atrophy and fasciculations. Acute lesions can differ from established patterns.

Study tip: hide this section, explain the concept aloud, then check whether you remembered the sequence and its rationale.

Clinical connection

Sudden focal weakness can be a stroke or other emergency. Seek urgent medical assessment rather than relying on a learning tool.
