Descending motor pathways and upper versus lower motor neuron lesions

26 min studyFree learning resourceIncludes memory aid

Lesson overview

Voluntary movement depends on motor cortex, descending pathways, spinal and cranial motor circuits, basal ganglia, cerebellum and sensory feedback. Corticospinal axons pass through the internal capsule, cerebral peduncles, pons and medullary pyramids. Most cross at the pyramidal decussation to form the lateral corticospinal tract. Upper motor neurons influence lower motor neurons, which directly innervate skeletal muscle. Upper motor neuron lesions classically cause weakness, increased tone and exaggerated reflexes after the acute phase; lower motor neuron lesions cause weakness with reduced tone and reflexes and may produce atrophy and fasciculations.

Learning objectives

  • Trace the corticospinal pathway.
  • Distinguish upper and lower motor neuron signs.
  • Relate lesion level to side of weakness.

High-yield points

  • A lesion above the pyramidal crossing commonly causes contralateral weakness; a lateral spinal cord lesion affects ipsilateral descending fibres below it.
  • Upper motor neuron signs often include spasticity, hyperreflexia and extensor plantar response.
  • Lower motor neuron signs include flaccid weakness, hyporeflexia, atrophy and fasciculations.
  • Movement also depends on corticobulbar, cerebellar, basal ganglia and sensory systems.

Memory aid / mnemonic

UMN = often increased tone/reflexes; LMN = reduced tone/reflexes, atrophy and fasciculations. Acute lesions can differ from established patterns.

Study tip: hide this section, explain the concept aloud, then check whether you remembered the sequence and its rationale.

Clinical connection

Sudden focal weakness can be a stroke or other emergency. Seek urgent medical assessment rather than relying on a learning tool.

Corticospinal pathway: from cortex to spinal motor circuits

Upper motor neuron cell bodies in motor and related cortical regions send axons through the corona radiata and internal capsule, then through the cerebral peduncles, pons and medullary pyramids. Most corticospinal fibres cross at the pyramidal decussation and descend in the lateral corticospinal tract; a smaller anterior corticospinal component has a different crossing pattern and often influences axial musculature bilaterally. Fibres ultimately influence spinal interneurons and motor neurons.

The corticobulbar pathway projects from cortex to motor nuclei of cranial nerves. Many cranial motor nuclei receive bilateral cortical input, but some muscles—classically the lower face and selected tongue movements—have stronger contralateral cortical control. Clinical patterns are therefore more nuanced than “all cranial nerves cross.”

Upper versus lower motor neuron signs

Upper motor neuron (UMN) lesions commonly produce weakness with increased tone, exaggerated deep tendon reflexes and an extensor plantar response after the acute phase; early spinal shock or acute lesions may initially reduce tone and reflexes. Lower motor neuron (LMN) lesions commonly produce weakness, reduced tone and reflexes, muscle wasting and sometimes fasciculations. These are patterns, not absolute rules, and timing and lesion site matter.

Always distinguish a tract lesion from a peripheral nerve lesion. A peripheral nerve lesion follows that nerve’s distribution and may involve sensory loss; a pure corticospinal lesion generally causes motor findings without a matching cutaneous sensory territory.

Self-test

  1. Where do most corticospinal fibres cross?
  2. Give three typical UMN findings.
  3. Why can an acute UMN lesion initially look flaccid?
Answers with reasoning

1. At the pyramidal decussation in the caudal medulla. 2. Weakness, increased tone, brisk reflexes and/or extensor plantar response. 3. Acute injury may transiently suppress spinal reflex activity before hyperreflexia develops.

Pathway reasoning: where does the lesion lie?

The lateral corticospinal tract crosses mainly at the pyramidal decussation in the caudal medulla. Therefore, a lesion in the cerebral hemisphere or brainstem above this crossing usually causes weakness on the opposite side of the body, whereas a lesion of the lateral corticospinal tract in the spinal cord usually affects the same side below the lesion. The anterior corticospinal system has a more variable bilateral influence, especially for axial musculature. Actual weakness patterns depend on lesion size, tract involvement and recovery stage.
