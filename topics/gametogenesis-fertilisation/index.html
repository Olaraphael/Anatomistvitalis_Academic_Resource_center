Gametogenesis, meiosis and fertilisation

20 min studyFree learning resourceIncludes memory aid

Lesson overview

Gametogenesis produces haploid sperm and oocytes. Meiosis reduces chromosome number and generates genetic variation through crossing-over and independent assortment. In oogenesis, primary oocytes begin meiosis before birth and pause in prophase I; meiosis II is completed only if fertilisation occurs. Fertilisation usually takes place in the ampulla of the uterine tube. Sperm capacitation and the acrosome reaction help sperm cross the oocyte coverings; membrane fusion activates the oocyte, helps prevent polyspermy and permits formation of a diploid zygote.

Learning objectives

  • Explain meiosis and genetic variation.
  • Compare spermatogenesis with oogenesis.
  • Summarise the major events of fertilisation.

High-yield points

  • Human gametes usually contain 23 chromosomes; the zygote usually contains 46.
  • Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes; meiosis II separates sister chromatids.
  • The cortical reaction helps reduce the chance of polyspermy.
  • Cleavage begins after the zygote forms.

Memory aid / mnemonic

Meiosis: “I = pairs, II = sisters.” Fertilisation: Capacitation → Penetration → Fusion → Oocyte activation → Pronuclei → Zygote.

Clinical connection

Chromosome segregation errors can lead to aneuploidy or pregnancy loss. Fertility concerns require individual clinical assessment.

Gametogenesis: why meiosis matters

Human somatic cells are diploid, with 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs. Gametes are haploid, with 23 chromosomes, so fertilisation restores the diploid chromosome number. Meiosis includes one round of DNA replication followed by two cell divisions. Crossing-over and independent assortment generate genetic variation; errors in chromosome segregation can produce aneuploid gametes.

Spermatogenesis occurs in seminiferous tubules. Spermatogonia give rise to primary spermatocytes, which undergo meiosis I to form secondary spermatocytes; meiosis II produces spermatids that remodel into spermatozoa during spermiogenesis. Sertoli cells support developing germ cells, while Leydig cells in the interstitium produce testosterone in response to LH.

Oogenesis begins before birth. Primary oocytes pause in prophase I; from puberty, selected oocytes resume meiosis. The cell released at ovulation is typically a secondary oocyte arrested in metaphase II. Meiosis II is completed if fertilisation occurs. Unequal cytokinesis preserves most cytoplasm in the oocyte and produces polar bodies.

Fertilisation: a logical sequence

  1. Sperm undergo capacitation in the female reproductive tract, enabling fertilising competence.
  2. Sperm interact with the cumulus cells and zona pellucida; the acrosomal reaction assists passage through surrounding barriers.
  3. One sperm fuses with the oocyte membrane and triggers events that reduce the chance of additional sperm entry.
  4. The secondary oocyte completes meiosis II.
  5. Maternal and paternal genetic material forms the zygote’s diploid genome, followed by cleavage.

Fertilisation usually occurs in the ampulla of the uterine tube. The zygote then travels toward the uterus while undergoing cleavage.

Exam distinctions

  • Meiosis I: homologous chromosomes separate; chromosome number is reduced.
  • Meiosis II: sister chromatids separate.
  • Ovulation: release of a secondary oocyte, not usually a completed ovum.
  • Spermiogenesis: transformation of spermatids into spermatozoa; it is not a meiotic division.
  • Capacitation: functional changes in sperm in the female tract; it is distinct from the acrosomal reaction.

Quick self-test

  1. Why must gametes be haploid?
  2. At what meiotic stage is the secondary oocyte arrested at ovulation?
  3. Where does fertilisation usually occur?
Reveal answers and explanations

1. To restore the diploid chromosome number at fertilisation rather than doubling it every generation. 2. Metaphase II. 3. The ampulla of the uterine tube.
