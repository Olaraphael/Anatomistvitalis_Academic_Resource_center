Cerebral blood supply and stroke localisation

24 min studyFree learning resourceIncludes memory aid

Lesson overview

The brain receives arterial blood through paired internal carotid and vertebral arteries. The vertebral arteries unite to form the basilar artery, supplying much of the brainstem, cerebellum and posterior circulation. The anterior and middle cerebral arteries arise from the internal carotid system; posterior cerebral arteries usually arise from the basilar bifurcation. The circle of Willis offers potential collateral connections, but collateral capacity varies and does not guarantee protection from ischaemia. Stroke localisation combines vascular territory with cortical function, visual pathways, brainstem signs and motor/sensory anatomy.

Learning objectives

  • Outline anterior and posterior cerebral circulations.
  • Relate broad ACA, MCA and PCA territories to typical findings.
  • Explain the limits of collateral circulation.

High-yield points

  • ACA lesions often affect medial frontal/parietal regions and may cause prominent contralateral leg weakness or sensory loss.
  • MCA lesions often affect lateral hemispheric regions; dominant-side lesions may affect language.
  • PCA lesions often affect occipital visual cortex and may cause contralateral visual field deficits.
  • Exact findings depend on the vessel, collateral flow and tissue involved.

Memory aid / mnemonic

ACA = leg area; MCA = lateral face/arm and dominant-hemisphere language; PCA = posterior visual cortex. These are broad patterns, not diagnostic rules.

Study tip: hide this section, explain the concept aloud, then check whether you remembered the sequence and its rationale.

Clinical connection

Sudden facial droop, arm weakness, speech difficulty, vision loss or severe imbalance requires emergency stroke assessment. Do not delay care to use a learning tool.

Localise a neurological deficit systematically

Neurological localisation is a reasoning process: define the deficit, identify the likely level, then consider causes. Start by distinguishing cortical, subcortical, brainstem, spinal cord, nerve-root, peripheral nerve, neuromuscular junction and muscle patterns. Distribution matters: face and arm involvement, aphasia, visual-field loss, crossed cranial-nerve/body findings, a sensory level, or weakness restricted to one nerve territory each suggests different anatomical possibilities.

Vascular territories: broad patterns

  • Middle cerebral artery: often affects lateral frontal/parietal regions; face and arm deficits may be prominent, and language or spatial-attention deficits depend on hemisphere and region.
  • Anterior cerebral artery: medial frontal/parietal regions; leg-predominant weakness or sensory loss can occur.
  • Posterior cerebral artery: occipital and inferomedial temporal regions; visual-field deficits are common patterns.
  • Brainstem circulation: may produce combinations of cranial nerve signs, long-tract deficits, ataxia or altered consciousness, depending on the site.

These are teaching patterns, not reliable self-diagnostic rules. Vascular anatomy varies and stroke syndromes can overlap.

Time-critical safety note

Sudden facial droop, arm weakness, speech difficulty, sudden vision loss, severe imbalance or an abrupt severe neurological symptom may indicate stroke or another emergency. Seek emergency medical care immediately; do not wait for symptoms to resolve or use a lesson to decide whether assessment is needed.

Self-test

  1. Why does the distribution of weakness matter for localisation?
  2. Which broad arterial territory is associated with many face-and-arm-predominant cortical deficits?
  3. What should someone do if stroke symptoms begin suddenly?
Answers with reasoning

1. Different levels and pathways supply different regions and functions. 2. Middle cerebral artery territory. 3. Seek emergency medical care immediately.
