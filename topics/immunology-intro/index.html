Introduction to immunology: innate and adaptive defence

The immune system distinguishes potentially harmful agents from normal tissues through layered defence. Innate immunity acts rapidly through epithelial barriers, phagocytes, natural killer cells, complement and inflammatory mediators. Adaptive immunity uses B lymphocytes and T lymphocytes to generate antigen-specific responses and memory. B cells can differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells; CD4 T cells coordinate responses, while CD8 T cells can kill infected or abnormal cells. Antigen presentation, cytokine signalling and immune regulation shape the response. Tolerance limits damaging reactions against self, but it is not perfect.

Learning objectives

  • Compare innate and adaptive immunity.
  • Describe broad functions of B cells, CD4 T cells and CD8 T cells.
  • Explain immune memory and the purpose of vaccination.

High-yield study points

  • Innate responses recognise broad molecular patterns and act quickly.
  • Adaptive responses are antigen-specific and can form long-lived memory.
  • Antibodies can neutralise targets, promote opsonisation and activate complement.
  • Major histocompatibility complex molecules present peptide antigens to T cells.
  • Immune regulation limits excessive inflammation and helps maintain self-tolerance.

Clinical connection

Vaccines train adaptive immune memory without requiring the learner to experience the full disease. Immune deficiencies, allergies and autoimmune disorders involve different failures or misdirection of immune function.
