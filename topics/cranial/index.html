The twelve cranial nerves

The twelve pairs of cranial nerves carry sensory, motor and parasympathetic information between the brain or brainstem and structures of the head, neck and selected thoracic and abdominal organs.

Explore lessons and quizzesBrowse all topic pages

Learning objectives

  • Name the twelve cranial nerves and recognise their broad sensory, motor and parasympathetic roles.
  • Distinguish nerves carrying special sensory information from those serving somatic motor or branchial motor functions.
  • Use a structured cranial nerve examination to relate findings to likely anatomical pathways.
  • Recognise that cranial nerve signs can arise from peripheral nerve, brainstem, neuromuscular or central lesions.

High-yield study points

  • I olfactory: smell; II optic: vision; III oculomotor: most eye movements, eyelid elevation and pupil constriction.
  • IV trochlear supplies superior oblique; V trigeminal provides facial sensation and muscles of mastication.
  • VI abducens supplies lateral rectus; VII facial controls facial expression and carries taste from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue.
  • VIII vestibulocochlear: hearing and balance; IX glossopharyngeal: posterior tongue taste and pharyngeal functions.
  • X vagus: palate, pharynx, larynx and extensive parasympathetic output; XI accessory: sternocleidomastoid and trapezius; XII hypoglossal: most tongue muscles.

Clinical connection

Cranial nerve examination links symptoms such as diplopia, facial weakness, dysphagia or tongue deviation with possible lesion sites.

Memory aid / mnemonic

“Some Say Marry Money, But My Brother Says Big Brains Matter More” is a first-pass sensory/motor cue for cranial nerves I–XII. Verify each nerve’s actual functions separately.

Revision tip: recall the cue without looking, then explain the anatomical reason behind it.

Clinical anatomy: cranial nerves by function and examination

Cranial nerves are best learned as pathways with nuclei, exits, targets and predictable deficits. The following is an educational screening framework; examination findings must be interpreted in context and do not establish a diagnosis alone.

CN I–VI: smell, vision and eye movement

I Olfactory: smell; receptor axons pass through the cribriform plate to the olfactory bulb and tract. II Optic: vision; fibres partially cross at the optic chiasm, where nasal retinal fibres decussate. A chiasmal lesion classically affects temporal visual fields bilaterally. III Oculomotor: most extraocular muscles, levator palpebrae and parasympathetic pupil constriction/accommodation; a significant palsy may cause ptosis, a “down-and-out” resting eye and sometimes a dilated pupil. IV Trochlear: superior oblique; helps depress the adducted eye. V Trigeminal: facial sensation and muscles of mastication; the corneal reflex afferent limb is V1. VI Abducens: lateral rectus abduction; long intracranial course makes it vulnerable to raised intracranial pressure and other lesions.

CN VII–XII: facial expression, hearing, swallowing and tongue

VII Facial: facial expression, taste from anterior two-thirds of tongue, and parasympathetic supply to lacrimal and selected salivary glands; efferent limb of corneal reflex. A peripheral facial palsy typically affects upper and lower face, while a supranuclear lesion often relatively spares forehead movement, though patterns are not absolute. VIII Vestibulocochlear: hearing and balance. IX Glossopharyngeal: taste and sensation from posterior third of tongue, pharyngeal sensation, stylopharyngeus, and parotid parasympathetic pathway; contributes to gag reflex afference. X Vagus: palate, pharynx and larynx motor functions, visceral sensory and parasympathetic output; contributes to gag reflex efference. XI Accessory: sternocleidomastoid and trapezius function. XII Hypoglossal: tongue movement; a lower motor neuron lesion often causes tongue deviation toward the weak side with wasting or fasciculations.

Reflexes help map pathways

The pupillary light reflex uses CN II afference, bilateral pretectal connections, Edinger–Westphal nuclei and CN III parasympathetic efference. The corneal reflex uses V1 afference and VII efference. The gag reflex involves IX afference and X efference, with considerable normal variability. These circuits illustrate why a reflex tests more than one nerve.

Optic pathway localisation

Retinal ganglion cell axons form the optic nerve; nasal retinal fibres cross at the chiasm while temporal retinal fibres remain ipsilateral. Behind the chiasm, each optic tract represents the contralateral visual hemifield. Fibres relay in the lateral geniculate nucleus and travel via optic radiations to primary visual cortex around the calcarine sulcus. Temporal-lobe Meyer’s loop carries superior visual-field information; parietal radiations carry inferior visual-field information. Lesions can produce characteristic field defects, but formal visual-field testing is needed to document them.

Self-test with reasoning

  1. Which nerves form the afferent and efferent limbs of the corneal reflex?
    Answer

    CN V1 is afferent and CN VII is efferent.

  2. Why can a lesion of the optic chiasm affect temporal fields of both eyes?
    Answer

    Nasal retinal fibres cross in the chiasm; these fibres carry information from the temporal visual fields.

  3. Which nerves are involved in the pupillary light reflex?
    Answer

    CN II carries the afferent signal and CN III carries parasympathetic efferent output to the sphincter pupillae, via the pretectal and Edinger–Westphal pathways.
