Respiratory physiology: ventilation, gas exchange and transport

Respiratory physiology describes movement of air into and out of the lungs, diffusion of gases across the alveolar-capillary membrane, and transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide in blood. Ventilation moves gas; perfusion brings blood to exchange surfaces; effective gas exchange requires their appropriate matching. Respiratory control adjusts ventilation in response to chemical and neural signals.

High-yield study points

  • In quiet inspiration, contraction of the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles increases thoracic volume and lowers alveolar pressure relative to atmosphere.
  • Airflow depends on pressure gradients and airway resistance; airway radius is a major determinant of resistance.
  • Gas diffusion depends on partial-pressure gradients, surface area, membrane thickness and gas properties.
  • Most oxygen is transported bound to haemoglobin; a smaller fraction is dissolved in plasma.
  • Carbon dioxide is transported dissolved, bound to proteins including haemoglobin, and predominantly as bicarbonate after reactions in red blood cells.
  • Ventilation-perfusion matching optimises exchange; low ventilation relative to perfusion and high ventilation relative to perfusion have different effects on gas exchange.
  • Central and peripheral chemoreceptors contribute to ventilatory control through responses to carbon dioxide, pH and oxygen, with sensitivity depending on physiological context.

Clinical connection

Low oxygen saturation, breathlessness and abnormal blood gases can arise from different mechanisms. A single symptom or number should be interpreted with examination and appropriate testing.
