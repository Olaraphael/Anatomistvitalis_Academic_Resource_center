Respiratory physiology: ventilation, gas exchange and transport

Respiratory physiology describes movement of air into and out of the lungs, diffusion of gases across the alveolar-capillary membrane, and transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide in blood. Ventilation moves gas; perfusion brings blood to exchange surfaces; effective gas exchange requires their appropriate matching. Respiratory control adjusts ventilation in response to chemical and neural signals.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish pulmonary ventilation, external respiration, gas transport and internal respiration.
  • Explain how pressure gradients and respiratory muscle activity drive airflow.
  • Relate alveolar ventilation and perfusion to effective gas exchange.
  • Describe how carbon dioxide transport links respiratory physiology with acid–base balance.

High-yield study points

  • In quiet inspiration, contraction of the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles increases thoracic volume and lowers alveolar pressure relative to atmosphere.
  • Airflow depends on pressure gradients and airway resistance; airway radius is a major determinant of resistance.
  • Gas diffusion depends on partial-pressure gradients, surface area, membrane thickness and gas properties.
  • Most oxygen is transported bound to haemoglobin; a smaller fraction is dissolved in plasma.
  • Carbon dioxide is transported dissolved, bound to proteins including haemoglobin, and predominantly as bicarbonate after reactions in red blood cells.
  • Ventilation-perfusion matching optimises exchange; low ventilation relative to perfusion and high ventilation relative to perfusion have different effects on gas exchange.
  • Central and peripheral chemoreceptors contribute to ventilatory control through responses to carbon dioxide, pH and oxygen, with sensitivity depending on physiological context.

Clinical connection

Low oxygen saturation, breathlessness and abnormal blood gases can arise from different mechanisms. A single symptom or number should be interpreted with examination and appropriate testing.

Check your understanding

  1. During quiet inspiration, what happens to thoracic volume and alveolar pressure?
    Reveal answer

    Thoracic volume increases and alveolar pressure falls slightly below atmospheric pressure, drawing air into the lungs.

  2. What does ventilation–perfusion matching help optimise?
    Reveal answer

    Efficient transfer of oxygen and carbon dioxide by matching regional airflow with pulmonary blood flow.

  3. Why can severe hypoventilation increase blood carbon dioxide?
    Reveal answer

    Less fresh gas reaches alveoli per unit time, reducing carbon dioxide elimination and potentially causing respiratory acidosis.

Separate the four stages of respiratory function

Ventilation moves air to and from alveoli; diffusion moves gases across the alveolar–capillary membrane; perfusion delivers blood to pulmonary capillaries; and transport carries gases in blood. Efficient exchange requires ventilation and perfusion to be appropriately matched. An alveolus with ventilation but little blood flow wastes ventilation; blood flowing past poorly ventilated alveoli leaves inadequately oxygenated.

During inspiration, the diaphragm contracts and thoracic volume increases, lowering alveolar pressure so air enters. Quiet expiration is usually passive due to elastic recoil. Pulmonary surfactant lowers surface tension and helps stabilise alveoli.

Minute ventilation versus alveolar ventilation

Minute ventilation = tidal volume × respiratory rate. Some inspired air remains in anatomical dead space and does not participate directly in gas exchange. Alveolar ventilation accounts for this dead space and is more closely related to carbon dioxide elimination. Rapid, shallow breathing can therefore increase minute ventilation without a proportional increase in effective alveolar ventilation.

Gas transport and control

Most oxygen is transported bound to haemoglobin. Increased temperature, increased carbon dioxide and lower pH shift the oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve to the right, favouring oxygen unloading in metabolically active tissues (the Bohr effect). Most carbon dioxide is transported as bicarbonate, formed through a reaction accelerated by carbonic anhydrase in red cells. Ventilation is a major rapid regulator of arterial carbon dioxide and contributes to acid–base balance.

Central and peripheral chemoreceptors respond to changes in carbon dioxide/pH and arterial oxygen, adjusting respiratory drive. The response is integrated with voluntary control, sleep, exercise and disease.

Self-test

  1. Distinguish ventilation from perfusion.
  2. Why is alveolar ventilation more useful than minute ventilation alone?
  3. What does a right shift of the oxygen–haemoglobin curve favour?
Answers with reasoning

1. Ventilation moves air; perfusion moves blood through pulmonary capillaries. 2. It accounts for dead space and reflects air reaching gas-exchanging alveoli. 3. Oxygen unloading to tissues.
