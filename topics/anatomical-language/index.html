Anatomical terminology and standard anatomical position

Anatomical terminology is a standard vocabulary used to describe location, relationship, orientation and movement without ambiguity. Every directional description assumes the standard anatomical position, even when a patient is sitting, lying down or turned during examination. The standard position is standing upright, head and eyes facing forward, upper limbs by the sides, palms facing forward, thumbs directed laterally, and feet flat and directed forward. Prone means lying face down; supine means lying face up.

Learning objectives

  • Use right and left from the subject’s perspective.
  • Define standard anatomical position and common directional terms.
  • Apply relational terms to describe structures precisely and unambiguously.

High-yield study points

  • Use right and left from the subject's own perspective, not the observer's. In an image, the patient's right may appear on the viewer's left.
  • Superior (cranial) means toward the head; inferior (caudal) means toward the feet or tail end.
  • Anterior (ventral) means toward the front; posterior (dorsal) means toward the back in the standard human description.
  • Medial means nearer the median plane; lateral means farther from it. Intermediate means between medial and lateral structures.
  • Proximal means nearer the trunk or point of origin; distal means farther away, especially along a limb or tubular structure.
  • Superficial means nearer the body surface; deep means farther from the surface. Internal and external describe relative positions within or outside a structure.
  • Ipsilateral means on the same side; contralateral means on the opposite side; bilateral means on both sides; unilateral means one side.
  • Palmar (volar) describes the palm of the hand; dorsal describes the back of the hand. Plantar describes the sole of the foot; dorsum describes its upper surface.

Clinical connection

A precise note such as 'a lesion on the left anterior forearm, distal to the elbow' is more useful than 'a mark below the arm'. Standard terms reduce errors in clinical handover, imaging interpretation and research.

Check your understanding

  1. In anatomical terminology, whose right and left are used?
    Reveal answer

    The subject’s right and left, not the observer’s.

  2. The sternum is ___ to the heart.
    Reveal answer

    Anterior (and relatively superficial to much of the heart).

  3. The wrist is ___ to the elbow in anatomical position.
    Reveal answer

    Distal, because it is farther from the limb’s point of attachment.

Using anatomical language precisely

Directional terms describe one structure in relation to another; they do not change when a patient turns, lies down or is viewed from a different side. Unless a question specifies otherwise, descriptions assume the standard anatomical position: standing erect, face and eyes forward, upper limbs by the sides, palms facing anteriorly, and feet directed forward. This standard reference frame prevents ambiguity in teaching, imaging and clinical communication.

Terms that are commonly confused

  • Medial and lateral: closer to or farther from the body's midline. The nose is medial to the eyes; the ears are lateral to the eyes.
  • Proximal and distal: nearer to or farther from the attachment or origin of a limb structure. The elbow is proximal to the wrist.
  • Superficial and deep: nearer to or farther from the body surface. The skin is superficial to skeletal muscle.
  • Anterior and posterior: toward the front or back of the body. In the thorax, the sternum is anterior to the heart and the vertebral column is posterior to it.
  • Ipsilateral and contralateral: on the same side or opposite sides. A right cerebral hemisphere lesion may cause left-sided weakness because major motor fibres cross.
  • Prone and supine: lying face downward and face upward. These describe body posture, not anatomical direction.

Clinical communication rule

“The lesion is on the left” is incomplete unless the writer makes clear whether left refers to the patient's anatomical left, a radiological display convention, or the observer's left. In imaging, confirm the side marker and modality conventions before interpreting laterality. In paired structures, specify the structure and side rather than relying on a gesture or screen position.

Short application

If a wound is on the lateral aspect of the right forearm, it lies on the side farther from the midline of the patient's right forearm. It does not mean the viewer's right side of a photograph. If the forearm is rotated, the anatomical relationship still depends on the standard reference frame and the named surface.

Worked examples: translate everyday descriptions into anatomical language

Example 1 — the thumb. In the standard anatomical position, the thumb is lateral to the little finger. Do not call it lateral simply because the hand is turned during examination: the reference is the standard position, not the current pose.

Example 2 — the sternum and heart. The sternum is anterior to the heart; the heart is posterior to the sternum. “The heart is behind the breastbone” is understandable everyday language, but the paired anatomical terms make the relationship more precise.

Example 3 — the elbow and wrist. The elbow is proximal to the wrist because it is nearer the limb's attachment to the trunk. The wrist is distal to the elbow. These terms describe relative position along a limb, not whether a structure is physically higher or lower in a photograph.

Example 4 — the skin and skeletal muscle. Skin is superficial to skeletal muscle, while skeletal muscle is deep to skin. “Superficial” and “deep” describe relative depth; they do not mean superior and inferior.

Example 5 — the lungs and mediastinum. The lungs are lateral to the mediastinum; the mediastinum is medial to each lung. “Medial” means nearer the median plane, not nearer the body's surface.

Common errors and how to correct them

  • Confusing the patient's right with the viewer's right: always use the subject's right and left. In a frontal clinical image, the patient's right may appear on the viewer's left.
  • Using superior/inferior for every limb relationship: use proximal/distal when describing distance along a limb, and use palmar/plantar or dorsal when the specific surface matters.
  • Treating prone and supine as anatomical positions: they are body positions. Standard anatomical position is the shared reference posture for terminology.
  • Using “medial” to mean “middle” loosely: identify the median plane and state whether the structure lies nearer to or farther from it.

Retrieval practice with explanations

1. Is the fibula medial or lateral to the tibia in anatomical position?

The fibula is lateral to the tibia. The tibia is the medial, weight-bearing bone of the leg; these relationships remain the same when the person changes posture.

2. A patient lies face upward. What is the position called?

Supine. Prone means face downward. These terms describe whole-body posture rather than directional relationships between individual structures.

3. Is the epidermis superficial or deep to the dermis?

The epidermis is superficial to the dermis. The dermis lies deeper and contains connective tissue, vessels, nerves and skin appendages.

4. Why is “the liver is on the right” an incomplete anatomical description?

It gives a broad side but not a precise relationship. A clearer description can identify the liver as predominantly in the right upper abdomen, inferior to the diaphragm and partly extending across the midline.

5. Which term describes the nose relative to the eyes?

Medial: the nose is nearer the median plane than the eyes. Lateral describes a position farther from the median plane.
