Skeletal anatomy: bone classification, landmarks and remodelling

The adult human skeleton is commonly described as 206 bones, although the count varies with sesamoid bones, accessory bones, age and counting conventions. The axial skeleton forms the central framework of the head, vertebral column and thoracic cage; the appendicular skeleton includes the limb bones and their girdles. Bones provide support, protect organs, act as levers, store minerals and contain marrow.

Learning objectives

High-yield study points

  • Long bones have a shaft and ends; short bones are roughly cube-shaped; flat bones provide broad surfaces; irregular bones have complex shapes; sesamoid bones develop within tendons.
  • Compact bone forms dense outer tissue; trabecular (spongy) bone contains a lattice of trabeculae and marrow spaces.
  • A typical long bone includes diaphysis, metaphyses, epiphyses, articular cartilage, periosteum, endosteum and a medullary cavity, with details varying by age and bone.
  • Common landmarks include processes, tubercles, tuberosities, crests, condyles, epicondyles, fossae, foramina, canals and notches; names often indicate shape or function.
  • Osteoblasts form matrix, osteoclasts resorb it and osteocytes maintain the living bone environment and respond to mechanical signals.
  • Bone remodelling balances formation and resorption; loading, hormones, nutrition, age and disease influence the process.
  • The periosteum is richly innervated and vascularised except over articular surfaces, which helps explain pain from periosteal injury.

Clinical connection

Fracture description should identify the bone, site, pattern, displacement and relevant joint or neurovascular risk. Bone density and healing depend on systemic health as well as the local injury.
