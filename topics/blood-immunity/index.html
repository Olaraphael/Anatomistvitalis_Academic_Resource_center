Blood, lymphatic anatomy and immune function

Blood is a specialised connective tissue composed of plasma and formed elements. The lymphatic system returns excess interstitial fluid to the circulation, transports absorbed lipids from the intestine and supports immune surveillance. Immune defence involves barriers, innate mechanisms and adaptive responses that recognise a wide range of threats while regulating damage to host tissues.

High-yield study points

  • Plasma contains water, electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, waste products and proteins including albumin, globulins and fibrinogen.
  • Erythrocytes transport oxygen through haemoglobin; leukocytes participate in immune defence; platelets contribute to haemostasis.
  • Innate immunity responds rapidly through barriers, complement, phagocytes, natural killer cells and inflammatory signalling.
  • Adaptive immunity uses B lymphocytes and antibodies and T lymphocytes with specialised effector and regulatory roles; memory can improve later responses.
  • Lymphatic capillaries collect excess interstitial fluid and transport lymph through vessels and nodes toward venous circulation.
  • Lymph nodes filter lymph and support immune-cell interactions; the spleen filters blood and participates in immune responses to blood-borne antigens.
  • Haemostasis balances vessel responses, platelet plug formation and coagulation with mechanisms that limit clotting to the appropriate site.

Clinical connection

Swollen lymph nodes may reflect infection, inflammation or other conditions and require interpretation in context. Blood counts and clotting tests describe different parts of physiology and are not interchangeable.
