Thoracic cavity: mediastinum, pleura and thoracic organs

The thoracic cavity contains two pleural cavities and the mediastinum. Each lung is covered by visceral pleura, while parietal pleura lines the thoracic wall, diaphragm and mediastinal surface. The mediastinum extends between the pleural cavities and contains the heart, great vessels, trachea, oesophagus, thymus and major nerves. The diaphragm separates thoracic and abdominal cavities and is the principal muscle of quiet inspiration. The thoracic wall includes ribs, sternum, thoracic vertebrae, intercostal muscles and neurovascular bundles that help protect organs and alter thoracic volume.

Learning objectives

  • Differentiate pleural cavities from the mediastinum.
  • Describe the pleural layers and the diaphragm.
  • Identify the major contents and boundaries of the thorax.

High-yield study points

  • The pleural cavity is a potential space containing a thin film of serous fluid.
  • The mediastinum is conventionally divided into superior and inferior parts; the inferior mediastinum has anterior, middle and posterior regions.
  • The phrenic nerves provide motor innervation to the diaphragm and carry sensory fibres from selected adjacent structures.
  • Intercostal neurovascular bundles generally run in the costal groove, with the main bundle arranged vein, artery, nerve from superior to inferior.
  • The oesophagus passes through the diaphragm at approximately T10 and the aorta at approximately T12; these are useful but simplified surface-level rules.

Clinical connection

Pneumothorax introduces air into the pleural space and can impair lung expansion. Pleural effusion, mediastinal masses and diaphragmatic dysfunction affect structures in different ways.

Check your understanding

  1. What are the three broad compartments of the mediastinum?
    Reveal answer

    Superior mediastinum and inferior mediastinum; the inferior mediastinum is subdivided into anterior, middle and posterior parts.

  2. Which pleural layer directly covers the lung surface?
    Reveal answer

    Visceral pleura.

  3. Why is the mediastinum clinically important?
    Reveal answer

    It contains the heart, great vessels, trachea, oesophagus, nerves and other structures, so disease or masses can affect multiple systems.

Thorax: compartment anatomy and clinically important relations

Mediastinal divisions

The transverse thoracic plane from the sternal angle to the T4/T5 intervertebral disc separates the superior mediastinum from the inferior mediastinum. The inferior mediastinum is divided into anterior, middle and posterior parts. The middle mediastinum contains the pericardium and heart; the posterior mediastinum contains the descending thoracic aorta, oesophagus, thoracic duct, azygos system and sympathetic trunks among other structures. The superior mediastinum contains the arch of the aorta and its branches, major veins, trachea, oesophagus, thymic tissue and nerves including the vagus and phrenic nerves.

Pleura and lungs

Visceral pleura closely invests the lung and enters the fissures; parietal pleura lines the thoracic wall, diaphragm and mediastinum. The pleural cavity is a potential space containing a thin lubricating fluid layer. The costodiaphragmatic recess is a dependent space into which the lung expands during deep inspiration and where fluid may collect. The right lung usually has three lobes and horizontal and oblique fissures; the left has two lobes, an oblique fissure, cardiac notch and lingula. Each bronchopulmonary segment is supplied by a segmental bronchus and a branch of the pulmonary artery, making segmental resection anatomically possible.

Heart, great vessels and nerves

The fibrous pericardium anchors the heart and limits acute over-expansion. The phrenic nerves pass anterior to the lung roots and provide motor supply to the diaphragm and sensory supply to fibrous pericardium and central diaphragmatic pleura. The vagus nerves pass posterior to the lung roots and contribute to pulmonary and oesophageal plexuses. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve loops under the aortic arch near the ligamentum arteriosum; enlargement of nearby structures can affect voice. The thoracic duct typically ascends in the posterior mediastinum and crosses toward the left venous angle, with anatomical variation.

Surface anatomy and emergencies

The sternal angle identifies the second costal cartilage and is a useful reference for rib counting. The tracheal bifurcation is near the transverse thoracic plane but varies with posture and respiration. A tension pneumothorax can impair venous return and cause obstructive shock; suspected tension pneumothorax is a clinical emergency. Pericardial tamponade restricts cardiac filling and can produce hypotension, raised venous pressure and muffled heart sounds, but diagnosis requires clinical assessment and usually imaging.
