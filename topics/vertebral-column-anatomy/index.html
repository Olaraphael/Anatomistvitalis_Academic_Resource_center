Vertebral column: regions, curves, discs and ligaments

The vertebral column supports the head and trunk, protects the spinal cord and transfers loads to the pelvis. It is usually described as seven cervical, twelve thoracic, five lumbar, five fused sacral and commonly four coccygeal vertebrae, with variation. Intervertebral discs between most adjacent vertebral bodies contain a fibrocartilaginous annulus fibrosus and a hydrated nucleus pulposus. Normal cervical and lumbar lordoses and thoracic and sacral kyphoses help distribute loads. Facet joints, ligaments and muscles guide and limit movement.

Learning objectives

  • Name the vertebral regions and typical counts.
  • Describe intervertebral disc structure and function.
  • Relate spinal curves and vertebral features to regional movement.

High-yield study points

  • Cervical vertebrae generally have transverse foramina; C1 and C2 are specialised for head support and rotation.
  • Thoracic vertebrae articulate with ribs and usually have longer, more inferiorly directed spinous processes.
  • Lumbar vertebrae have large bodies adapted for weight-bearing.
  • The posterior longitudinal ligament lies inside the vertebral canal along vertebral bodies and discs; the ligamentum flavum connects adjacent laminae.
  • Disc herniation effects depend on level, direction and the neural structures involved.

Clinical connection

Back pain is multifactorial. Red flags such as progressive neurological deficits, major trauma, fever or bladder/bowel dysfunction require prompt clinical assessment.

Check your understanding

  1. How many cervical, thoracic and lumbar vertebrae are typical in an adult?
    Reveal answer

    Seven cervical, twelve thoracic and five lumbar vertebrae, followed by the sacrum and coccyx; anatomical variation occurs.

  2. What is the main role of intervertebral discs?
    Reveal answer

    They distribute load, permit movement and absorb compressive forces between most adjacent vertebral bodies.

  3. Why is a herniated disc clinically important?
    Reveal answer

    Disc material can irritate or compress nearby neural structures, producing pain or neurological deficits depending on location and extent.

Check your understanding: vertebral column

  1. Which features distinguish a typical cervical vertebra from a typical thoracic vertebra?
    Answer and explanation

    Typical cervical vertebrae have transverse foramina and often a bifid spinous process; thoracic vertebrae have costal facets for rib articulation and typically longer, downward-sloping spinous processes. C1, C2, C7 and transitional vertebrae are atypical.

  2. Why is the intervertebral disc important mechanically?
    Answer and explanation

    The annulus fibrosus and gelatinous nucleus pulposus distribute compressive load and permit controlled movement between vertebral bodies.

  3. What is the main anatomical distinction between the spinal cord and cauda equina in adults?
    Answer and explanation

    The spinal cord usually ends around L1–L2 vertebral level in adults, while lumbar, sacral and coccygeal roots continue downward in the vertebral canal as the cauda equina.

Regional anatomy and clinical relationships

The vertebral column has seven cervical, twelve thoracic and five lumbar vertebrae, followed by the sacrum and coccyx. The number of vertebrae can vary, and transitional vertebrae may complicate numbering; imaging reports should use a clearly defined counting method when precise levels matter.

Intervertebral discs and ligaments

Most adjacent vertebral bodies are separated by fibrocartilaginous discs with an outer annulus fibrosus and a hydrated nucleus pulposus. The discs distribute load and permit controlled movement. The anterior longitudinal ligament limits excessive extension; the posterior longitudinal ligament lies within the vertebral canal along the posterior vertebral bodies; ligamenta flava connect adjacent laminae; and interspinous, supraspinous and intertransverse ligaments help limit particular movements.

Curvatures and load distribution

Thoracic and sacral curves are primary curvatures; cervical and lumbar curves develop as posture and locomotor control mature. The curves help distribute loads and maintain balance. Excessive curvature can have multiple causes, including development, degeneration, neuromuscular disease and structural changes.

Intervertebral foramina

Adjacent vertebral notches form each intervertebral foramen, through which a spinal nerve and associated vessels pass. Disc height, facet orientation, osteophytes and other changes can alter foraminal dimensions. A nerve-root syndrome should be correlated with the relevant level, dermatome, myotome, reflex and imaging findings rather than inferred from back pain alone.
