Biomedical research literacy: evidence, citations and figures

Biomedical learning improves when claims are matched to appropriate evidence. Textbooks are useful for established foundations; systematic reviews synthesise research; original studies answer narrower questions; and clinical guidelines translate evidence into recommendations for defined settings. Evaluate the research question, study design, sample, comparison group, measurements, uncertainty, conflicts of interest and limitations. Association does not automatically prove causation, and statistical significance does not guarantee clinical importance. When using diagrams, tables or images, record the source and licence and distinguish your own interpretation from the source's claims.

Learning objectives

  • Identify common biomedical source types and what each can support.
  • Evaluate a research claim using design, sample and uncertainty.
  • Apply ethical citation and figure-reuse practices.

High-yield study points

  • Prefer recent, relevant primary research and high-quality reviews for current questions, while retaining foundational sources when necessary.
  • Randomisation and appropriate comparison groups can reduce some biases but do not eliminate all limitations.
  • Confidence intervals convey uncertainty; p-values alone do not describe effect size or clinical importance.
  • Citations should let readers locate the original source and verify the claim.
  • Copyright and open licences differ; online availability does not mean unrestricted reuse.

Clinical connection

A balanced evidence appraisal reduces the risk of overstating preliminary findings. Educational content should separate established knowledge, emerging evidence and hypotheses.
