Bone classification, landmarks and structural organisation

Bones are classified by shape as long, short, flat, irregular, sesamoid or pneumatic, although some bones combine features. A typical long bone has a shaft (diaphysis), ends (epiphyses), metaphyses, a medullary cavity and articular surfaces. Compact bone forms a dense outer layer; cancellous bone contains trabeculae that distribute load and house marrow spaces. Periosteum covers most external bone surfaces except articular regions, while endosteum lines internal surfaces. Bone markings reflect muscle attachment, tendon passage, joint formation and neurovascular routes.

Learning objectives

  • Classify bones by shape and give examples.
  • Identify the major regions of a long bone.
  • Interpret common bone surface markings.

High-yield study points

  • A tubercle, tuberosity, crest or line commonly indicates an attachment site, but naming conventions are not perfectly uniform.
  • A fossa is a depression; a foramen is an opening; a canal or meatus is a passage.
  • The epiphyseal plate permits longitudinal growth before closure; its remnant is the epiphyseal line.
  • Compact bone is organised into osteons in many regions; trabecular bone aligns broadly with mechanical stresses.
  • Bone marrow may be red and haematopoietic or yellow and rich in adipose tissue; distribution changes with age.

Clinical connection

Fracture descriptions should identify the bone, exact location, pattern, displacement, skin involvement and nearby neurovascular risk where known.
