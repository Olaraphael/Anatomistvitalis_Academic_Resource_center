Head anatomy: an overview

The head contains the brain and special sense organs and provides entrances to the respiratory and digestive tracts. Its framework includes the neurocranium, which protects the brain, and the viscerocranium, which supports the face.

Learning objectives

  • Organise the head into neurocranial, facial, orbital, nasal, oral and related regions.
  • Relate the cranial cavity to the brain and identify the major sensory and digestive entry regions of the face.
  • Explain why the skull, cranial nerves, vessels and fascial spaces must be studied together.
  • Use regional anatomy to structure a systematic head examination.

High-yield study points

  • The neurocranium encloses the cranial cavity; the viscerocranium forms much of the facial skeleton.
  • The scalp is classically described in five layers: skin, dense connective tissue, aponeurosis, loose areolar tissue and pericranium.
  • The cranial cavity contains the brain, meninges, vessels and proximal parts of cranial nerves.
  • The orbit, nasal cavity and oral cavity have specialised functions and complex anatomical relationships.
  • Study head and neck regionally: bones, muscles, nerves, vessels and spaces.

Clinical connection

Scalp wounds can bleed heavily because vessels are held open by dense connective tissue. Neurological symptoms after head trauma require urgent assessment.

Check your understanding

  1. What is the principal function of the neurocranium?
    Reveal answer

    To enclose and protect the brain and associated intracranial structures.

  2. Which cranial nerve provides most general sensation from the face?
    Reveal answer

    The trigeminal nerve (CN V), through its ophthalmic, maxillary and mandibular divisions.

  3. Why are foramina clinically important?
    Reveal answer

    They provide passages for cranial nerves and vessels, so lesions or fractures around them may produce predictable deficits.

Check your understanding: head anatomy

  1. Why are the cranial cavity, orbit, nasal cavity and oral cavity studied as connected spaces?
    Answer and explanation

    They communicate through defined openings and are closely related to neurovascular, respiratory, digestive and sensory structures. Infections, trauma and tumours may spread along these anatomical routes.

  2. What is the functional importance of the cavernous sinus region?
    Answer and explanation

    It is related to the internal carotid artery and several cranial nerves controlling eye movement and facial sensation. Pathology in this region can therefore produce combined ocular motor and sensory findings.

  3. Why must facial sensory and motor innervation be distinguished?
    Answer and explanation

    Most facial expression muscles are supplied by the facial nerve (VII), while cutaneous sensation of most of the face is carried by the trigeminal nerve (V), with important exceptions around the angle of the mandible and external ear.
