Radiological anatomy: X-ray, CT, MRI and ultrasound

Radiological anatomy studies body structure through imaging in living people. Each modality measures different physical properties, so an image is not a direct photograph of anatomy. Image interpretation starts with patient identity and orientation, modality, plane, contrast, normal landmarks and the question being asked.

High-yield study points

  • Plain radiography uses X-rays; dense structures such as bone usually appear radiopaque compared with air, while soft-tissue contrast is more limited.
  • CT uses multiple X-ray measurements reconstructed into cross-sectional images and is particularly useful for many acute injuries and thoracic or abdominal assessments.
  • MRI uses magnetic fields and radiofrequency signals and offers excellent soft-tissue contrast; sequences alter the appearance of water, fat, blood and pathology.
  • Ultrasound uses reflected sound waves, permits dynamic examination and avoids ionising radiation; image quality depends on the acoustic window and operator technique.
  • Sagittal, coronal and axial planes should be identified before interpreting relationships; the displayed left-right convention varies by view and modality.
  • Contrast agents can improve visualisation of vessels, organs or lesions but have indications, contraindications and risks that require trained clinical judgement.
  • A normal variant, artefact or partial-volume effect can mimic disease; findings should be interpreted with the clinical history and other evidence.

Clinical connection

This lesson introduces image orientation, not independent diagnosis. Clinical imaging interpretation requires formal training, appropriate supervision and current protocols.
