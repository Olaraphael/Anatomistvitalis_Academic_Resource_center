Pelvic blood vessels, lymphatics and autonomic nerves

A regional anatomy lesson on internal iliac branches, pelvic venous drainage, lymphatic routes, sacral plexus and autonomic pelvic plexuses.

Return to pelvis & perineum courseBrowse all topics

1. Internal iliac artery: organisation

The common iliac artery usually divides into external and internal iliac arteries near the pelvic brim. The external iliac continues beneath the inguinal ligament as the femoral artery; the internal iliac supplies much of the pelvis, gluteal region and perineum. Branch patterns are variable, and “anterior” and “posterior” division groupings are useful teaching schemes rather than absolutely identical arrangements in every person.

The posterior division commonly gives iliolumbar, lateral sacral and superior gluteal branches. The anterior division commonly gives umbilical/superior vesical, obturator, inferior vesical in males or vaginal in females, uterine in females, middle rectal, internal pudendal and inferior gluteal branches, with variable origins. The superior gluteal artery usually leaves the pelvis above piriformis; most other major gluteal neurovascular structures pass below it. The internal pudendal artery leaves through the greater sciatic foramen, hooks around the ischial spine/sacrospinous ligament region, and enters the perineum through the lesser sciatic foramen.

2. Venous drainage and haemorrhage

Pelvic veins form interconnected plexuses around the bladder, prostate, uterus, vagina and rectum. These drain toward internal iliac tributaries, but cross-connections and valveless channels mean spread of blood or disease does not always follow a single textbook route. Pelvic venous plexuses can bleed significantly during trauma or surgery. The internal iliac system also communicates with vertebral venous pathways, a clinically relevant route in some disease processes.

Pelvic fractures can damage arterial branches or venous plexuses. Suspected pelvic-ring injury with shock is an emergency; definitive assessment may require trauma imaging, haemorrhage control and multidisciplinary care. A non-specialist should not attempt to diagnose or manage internal bleeding from symptoms alone.

3. Sacral plexus and somatic nerves

The sacral plexus is formed from the lumbosacral trunk (L4–L5) and anterior rami of S1–S4 on the pelvic wall, typically anterior to piriformis. Major branches include the sciatic nerve, superior and inferior gluteal nerves, posterior femoral cutaneous nerve, pudendal nerve and nerves to obturator internus and quadratus femoris. Branches to pelvic muscles and skin show anatomical variation. The obturator nerve arises from L2–L4 and passes along the lateral pelvic wall to the medial thigh; it is not a branch of the sacral plexus.

The pudendal nerve arises mainly from S2–S4, leaves the pelvis via the greater sciatic foramen, passes near the ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament, and enters the perineum via the lesser sciatic foramen. It supplies somatic sensation to much of the external genital/perineal region and motor fibres to external urethral and anal sphincter complexes and other perineal muscles. The pudendal nerve is not the sole nerve supplying every pelvic-floor muscle; levator ani receives direct branches from sacral roots and other variable contributions.

4. Autonomic innervation

Sympathetic pathways reach the pelvis through hypogastric nerves and pelvic plexuses, carrying vasomotor and visceral motor fibres and contributing to reproductive functions. Parasympathetic pelvic splanchnic nerves arise from S2–S4 and contribute to detrusor contraction, rectal motility and erectile tissue responses. The inferior hypogastric (pelvic) plexus lies beside pelvic viscera and distributes autonomic fibres through organ-specific plexuses. Visceral pain afferents may travel with sympathetic or parasympathetic pathways depending on the organ and clinical context; referred pain patterns are therefore not simple one-to-one maps.

Bladder filling and voiding require coordinated detrusor activity, outlet relaxation, sphincter control and higher-centre modulation. Bowel continence and defaecation similarly require integration of rectal sensation, internal and external sphincters, pelvic floor and voluntary control. Pelvic nerve injury can affect bladder, bowel, sexual function and sensation in combinations that depend on lesion location.

5. Lymphatic drainage: broad patterns

Pelvic lymph generally drains to internal, external and common iliac nodes, with sacral nodes receiving drainage from posterior pelvic structures. Gonadal lymphatics typically follow gonadal vessels to para-aortic/lumbar nodes rather than simply draining to the nearby pelvic nodes; this reflects embryological origin and is important in oncological staging. The exact routes vary by organ, subregion and disease. Lymph-node pathways are useful for understanding spread but do not establish a diagnosis or stage cancer without clinical investigations.

Self-test

  1. What is the main arterial source for most pelvic viscera?
    Answer

    The internal iliac artery and its branches, with important variations and contributions depending on the organ.

  2. What roots form the pudendal nerve?
    Answer

    Mainly S2–S4.

  3. Where do gonadal lymphatics commonly drain?
    Answer

    Para-aortic/lumbar nodes, reflecting the gonads’ embryological ascent.

Study and safety note

This is a learning resource for anatomy study, not a substitute for supervised dissection, clinical training or professional medical judgement. Anatomical patterns vary; clinical symptoms must be interpreted by qualified professionals.

Pelvic neurovascular anatomy: tracing routes, territories and injury patterns

Internal iliac artery as a branching system

The internal iliac artery usually divides into posterior and anterior divisions, but the branching pattern varies. The posterior division commonly supplies the iliolumbar region, sacral canal and gluteal region through iliolumbar, lateral sacral and superior gluteal arteries. The anterior division gives visceral and parietal branches, including superior vesical, obturator, uterine or inferior vesical, vaginal, middle rectal, internal pudendal and inferior gluteal branches in variable arrangements. A reliable approach is to learn the territory and course of each vessel, then learn common origins; memorising one diagram as if every body is identical can be misleading.

The internal pudendal artery exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, passes near the ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament, and enters the perineum through the lesser sciatic foramen. It then travels in the pudendal canal with the pudendal nerve. This route explains why the ischial spine is a landmark for pudendal nerve block and why pelvic fractures or deep perineal injury may affect pudendal vessels.

Venous plexuses and haemorrhage

Pelvic veins form interconnected plexuses around the bladder, prostate, uterus, vagina and rectum. These channels communicate with internal iliac tributaries and may be valveless or have variable valve function, allowing pressure and flow patterns to differ from a simple arterial tree. Venous plexuses around pelvic organs can bleed substantially during trauma or surgery. The presacral venous plexus is particularly important because large veins lie against the sacrum and may be difficult to control if injured.

Lymphatic drainage by organ region

Lymph follows vessels but does not always drain to the nearest palpable node. Most pelvic viscera drain to internal iliac, external iliac, obturator, sacral or common iliac nodal groups depending on the organ and subregion. Gonadal lymphatics generally follow the gonadal vessels to para-aortic nodes because the gonads develop high on the posterior abdominal wall before descending. This developmental fact explains why testicular and ovarian lymph drainage differs from that of many pelvic organs. The anal canal also has a clinically important transition: lymphatic drainage above and below the pectinate line differs.

Sacral plexus and autonomic pathways

The sacral plexus is formed from the lumbosacral trunk and anterior rami of S1–S4, lying on the anterior surface of piriformis. It gives rise to the sciatic, superior and inferior gluteal, posterior femoral cutaneous and pudendal nerves, among other branches. Pelvic splanchnic nerves from S2–S4 carry parasympathetic fibres to pelvic viscera and distal hindgut; lumbar splanchnic pathways contribute sympathetic fibres that reach the superior and inferior hypogastric plexuses. The inferior hypogastric plexus is a mixed autonomic network supplying bladder, rectum and reproductive organs. Visceral pain pathways may travel with sympathetic or parasympathetic routes depending on the organ and pain type, so referred pain patterns require clinical context.

How to trace a nerve on a specimen or scan

  1. Start at its spinal roots or plexus of origin.
  2. Identify its relationship to a fixed landmark such as piriformis, the sacral foramina, ischial spine or obturator internus.
  3. Follow the nerve through each opening or canal; do not assume a nerve that exits the pelvis remains outside it.
  4. Connect the course to motor, sensory and autonomic functions, then predict the deficits of a lesion at each level.

Clinical application: new saddle sensory loss, urinary retention or incontinence, bilateral leg weakness, or loss of anal tone can indicate cauda equina or other urgent neurological pathology and requires emergency assessment.
