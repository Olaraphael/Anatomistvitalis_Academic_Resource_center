Special senses: eye, ear, smell and taste anatomy

Special senses rely on specialised receptors and neural pathways. The eye has fibrous, vascular and neural coats: sclera and cornea; choroid, ciliary body and iris; and retina. Light passes through the cornea, aqueous humour, pupil, lens and vitreous body before reaching the retina. The ear includes an external sound-collecting region, a middle ear that transmits vibrations through the ossicles, and an inner ear containing the cochlea and vestibular apparatus. Olfactory receptors occupy the superior nasal cavity; taste receptor cells are located in taste buds associated with papillae and other oral surfaces.

Learning objectives

  • Trace the passage of light through the eye.
  • Distinguish external, middle and inner ear structures.
  • Describe the receptor locations for smell and taste.

High-yield study points

  • The cornea provides much of the eye's refractive power; the lens changes shape for accommodation.
  • The retina converts light into neural signals; the optic nerve carries output toward the brain.
  • The auditory ossicles are malleus, incus and stapes.
  • The cochlea supports hearing; semicircular ducts and otolith organs contribute to balance.
  • Smell and taste are chemical senses that interact strongly in flavour perception.

Clinical connection

Sudden visual loss, severe eye pain, sudden hearing loss or acute neurological symptoms need urgent medical assessment. Anatomical knowledge supports understanding but does not replace examination.

Check your understanding: special senses

  1. Where are photoreceptors located?
    Answer and explanation

    In the neural retina. Rods are highly sensitive in dim light; cones support colour vision and high spatial acuity, especially in the fovea.

  2. What is the role of the organ of Corti?
    Answer and explanation

    It contains sensory hair cells in the cochlea that transduce sound-related mechanical movement into neural signals carried by the cochlear division of cranial nerve VIII.

  3. Which inner-ear structures detect angular acceleration and which detect linear acceleration/head tilt?
    Answer and explanation

    The semicircular ducts detect angular acceleration; the utricle and saccule detect linear acceleration and head position relative to gravity.

  4. Why is smell anatomically unusual among sensory pathways?
    Answer and explanation

    Olfactory receptor neurons project to the olfactory bulb and olfactory cortical regions through a pathway that reaches primary olfactory cortex without the same initial thalamic relay characteristic of most other sensory systems.

Special senses: receptor anatomy to central pathways

Eye and visual pathway

The cornea supplies much of the eye’s refractive power; the lens changes shape during accommodation under parasympathetic control of the ciliary muscle. The iris controls pupil diameter. Aqueous humour flows from the posterior chamber through the pupil into the anterior chamber and drains mainly through the trabecular meshwork and canal of Schlemm. Imbalance between production and drainage can elevate intraocular pressure, although glaucoma is a group of optic neuropathies and is not diagnosed by pressure alone.

The retina contains photoreceptors, bipolar cells, horizontal and amacrine interneurones, and ganglion cells. Rods are highly sensitive in dim light; cones support colour and high-acuity vision, especially in the fovea. Ganglion-cell axons form the optic nerve. At the optic chiasm, fibres from the nasal halves of both retinas cross, while temporal-retinal fibres remain ipsilateral. The optic tracts therefore carry information about the contralateral visual field to the lateral geniculate nucleus, then via optic radiations to the primary visual cortex around the calcarine sulcus. Temporal-loop fibres carry part of the superior visual field representation; parietal radiations carry part of the inferior field representation.

Ear: hearing

The auricle and external acoustic meatus direct sound to the tympanic membrane. The malleus, incus and stapes transmit and amplify vibration across the middle ear to the oval window. Movement of cochlear fluids displaces the basilar membrane; hair-cell stereocilia bend against the mechanical environment of the organ of Corti, converting movement into receptor potentials. The cochlear division of CN VIII carries auditory information through brainstem nuclei, bilateral ascending pathways, the inferior colliculus and medial geniculate nucleus to auditory cortex. Because central auditory projections are bilateral, a unilateral central lesion rarely causes complete deafness in one ear.

Vestibular apparatus

The semicircular ducts detect angular acceleration through movement of endolymph and deflection of the cupula in each ampulla. The utricle and saccule contain otolith organs that respond to linear acceleration and head tilt relative to gravity. Vestibular afferents project to vestibular nuclei and cerebellum, which coordinate vestibulo-ocular reflexes, posture and balance. The vestibulo-ocular reflex stabilises gaze during head movement by producing compensatory eye movement in the opposite direction.

Smell and taste

Olfactory receptor neurones lie in specialised epithelium high in the nasal cavity. Their axons pass through the cribriform plate to the olfactory bulb and then project to primary olfactory regions, with strong connections to limbic and orbitofrontal networks. Taste receptor cells in taste buds detect chemical stimuli that contribute to sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami qualities. Taste information travels mainly through cranial nerves VII, IX and X to the nucleus of the solitary tract, then through thalamic relays to gustatory cortex. What people call “flavour” also depends heavily on smell, texture, temperature and trigeminal sensations.

Clinical localisation and limits

  • Optic nerve lesions affect input from one eye; optic-chiasm lesions may cause bitemporal field loss; post-chiasmal lesions typically affect the contralateral visual field.
  • Conductive hearing loss involves external/middle-ear sound transmission; sensorineural loss involves cochlear hair cells, auditory nerve or central auditory structures.
  • Vertigo, imbalance and oscillopsia can arise from peripheral or central vestibular disorders; red flags such as sudden neurological symptoms require urgent assessment.
  • Loss of smell can follow viral illness, head injury, nasal disease or neurological conditions; persistent or sudden symptoms deserve appropriate evaluation.
