Blood: formed elements and plasma

Blood is a specialised fluid connective tissue that transports gases, nutrients, metabolites, hormones and heat; supports immune defence; and contributes to haemostasis and pH regulation. Centrifuged blood separates into plasma and formed elements. Plasma is mostly water and contains electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, waste products and proteins including albumin, globulins and fibrinogen. Erythrocytes contain haemoglobin for oxygen transport and contribute to carbon dioxide transport and buffering. Leukocytes participate in innate and adaptive immunity: neutrophils are important phagocytes, monocytes can become macrophages, lymphocytes include B cells, T cells and natural killer cells, and eosinophils and basophils participate in selected inflammatory and immune responses. Platelets are cell fragments derived from megakaryocytes and contribute to primary haemostasis; coagulation proteins stabilise the developing clot. A complete blood count measures selected cell counts and indices but does not independently identify every cause of an abnormal result. Interpretation depends on symptoms, reference intervals, specimen quality and additional tests.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the major components and functions of blood.
  • Compare erythrocytes, leukocytes and platelets.
  • Explain the difference between plasma and serum in broad terms.

High-yield study points

  • Plasma contains water, electrolytes and proteins including albumin and fibrinogen.
  • Erythrocytes transport oxygen using haemoglobin.
  • Leukocytes support innate and adaptive immune defence.
  • Platelets and coagulation proteins cooperate in haemostasis.
  • Serum is the fluid remaining after clotting and therefore lacks most consumed fibrinogen.

Clinical connection

Anaemia, infection, inflammation and bleeding disorders can alter blood results in different patterns. Laboratory values should be interpreted by a qualified clinician with the clinical context.
