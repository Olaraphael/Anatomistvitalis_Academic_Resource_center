Bone tissue and remodelling

Bone is living mineralised connective tissue. Its extracellular matrix contains type I collagen and mineral, mainly hydroxyapatite. Compact and trabecular bone differ in organisation, but both undergo continuous remodelling.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the gross and cellular organisation of bone tissue.
  • Distinguish osteoblasts, osteocytes and osteoclasts by function.
  • Compare compact and cancellous bone and relate their organisation to mechanical loading.
  • Explain how remodelling contributes to mineral homeostasis and adaptation to stress.

High-yield study points

  • Osteoblasts form bone matrix and support mineralisation.
  • Osteoclasts resorb bone.
  • Osteocytes are mature cells embedded in matrix and help sense mechanical loading.
  • Remodelling supports repair, mineral homeostasis and adaptation to loading.
  • Bone growth, modelling and remodelling are related but distinct processes.

Clinical connection

Bone remodelling responds to mechanical forces, hormones, age, nutrition and disease. Bone is living tissue, not an inert scaffold.

Check your understanding: bone tissue

  1. Which bone cell produces osteoid?
    Answer and explanation

    Osteoblasts synthesise osteoid, the organic bone matrix, which later mineralises. Osteocytes are mature cells embedded in matrix, and osteoclasts resorb bone.

  2. What is the key difference between compact and spongy bone?
    Answer and explanation

    Compact bone is organised into dense osteons in many locations; spongy bone consists of trabeculae with marrow spaces. Both contain lamellar bone in mature healthy tissue.

  3. Why is bone considered a dynamic tissue?
    Answer and explanation

    Bone is continuously remodelled in response to mechanical loading, hormones, mineral needs and microdamage. Formation and resorption must be coordinated to preserve strength and mineral homeostasis.

Bone tissue: cellular biology, mechanics and remodelling

Matrix and structural organisation

Bone is a specialised mineralised connective tissue. Type I collagen and other matrix proteins provide tensile strength and a framework for mineral deposition; hydroxyapatite crystals provide stiffness and resistance to compression. Compact (cortical) bone is organised into osteons in many regions, with concentric lamellae around central canals carrying vessels and nerves. Trabecular (cancellous) bone consists of interconnected plates and rods aligned broadly with loading patterns; marrow occupies the intervening spaces. Both are living, vascular tissues and both remodel continuously.

Bone cells and their roles

Osteoblasts arise from osteogenic precursor cells and produce osteoid, the unmineralised organic matrix. Mineralisation follows under regulated conditions. Some osteoblasts become osteocytes embedded in lacunae; their processes run through canaliculi, enabling communication and mechanosensing within the matrix. Osteoclasts are multinucleated cells derived from the monocyte/macrophage lineage; they resorb bone by acidifying a sealed resorption compartment and releasing enzymes that degrade matrix. Bone-lining cells cover inactive surfaces and participate in surface regulation.

Modelling, remodelling and fracture repair

Bone modelling changes bone shape or size when formation and resorption occur at different surfaces, especially during growth and adaptation. Remodelling replaces old or damaged tissue through coupled osteoclast resorption and osteoblast formation. It helps repair microdamage and regulate calcium and phosphate balance. After fracture, healing may proceed by direct bone healing under very stable conditions or by callus formation when controlled movement occurs. Healing is influenced by blood supply, stability, fracture gap, infection, nutrition, age and systemic disease.

Hormonal and mechanical regulation

Parathyroid hormone, vitamin D, calcitonin, sex steroids and local signalling pathways influence bone turnover and mineral metabolism. Osteocytes detect mechanical strain and regulate signals that affect osteoblast and osteoclast activity. Habitual loading supports maintenance of bone, while prolonged unloading can reduce bone mass. Excessive or repetitive loading without adequate recovery can produce stress injury. Calcium intake alone cannot explain bone health; vitamin D status, protein, overall nutrition, hormones, renal function and activity also matter.

Clinical correlations

  • Osteoporosis: reduced bone strength due to changes in bone quantity and microarchitecture, increasing fracture risk.
  • Osteomalacia/rickets: impaired mineralisation of newly formed bone, often related to vitamin D or phosphate disorders.
  • Osteomyelitis: infection of bone that can impair local tissue and blood supply.
  • Paget disease: disorganised high-turnover remodelling producing structurally abnormal bone.
  • Delayed union/non-union: failure of expected fracture healing, requiring assessment of mechanical and biological factors.

These conditions have different mechanisms and should not be treated as interchangeable forms of “weak bone”.
