Haemostasis: platelets, coagulation and fibrinolysis

Haemostasis limits blood loss after vascular injury while preserving flow elsewhere. Vasoconstriction is followed by platelet adhesion, activation and aggregation to form a primary platelet plug. Coagulation reactions generate thrombin, which converts fibrinogen to fibrin; fibrin stabilises the plug and is cross-linked. Natural anticoagulant systems limit clot growth, while fibrinolysis later breaks down fibrin through plasmin activity. In vivo, these processes are coordinated on cell surfaces and regulated by vessel integrity, blood flow and biochemical feedback rather than operating as two isolated linear cascades.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish primary haemostasis from fibrin stabilisation.
  • Explain the roles of platelets, thrombin, fibrin and fibrinolysis.
  • Identify factors that influence bleeding and thrombosis risk.

High-yield study points

  • Platelets adhere to exposed subendothelial structures through adhesion proteins and become activated.
  • Thrombin converts fibrinogen into fibrin and activates several components that amplify coagulation.
  • Vitamin K is required for normal function of several coagulation factors.
  • Antithrombin, tissue factor pathway inhibitor and the protein C system help restrain coagulation.
  • Plasmin breaks down fibrin; D-dimer can reflect fibrin turnover but is not specific to one diagnosis.

Clinical connection

Unusual bleeding, suspected thrombosis or anticoagulant-related concerns require clinical assessment. Coagulation tests measure selected aspects of the system and do not capture every bleeding or clotting disorder.
