Autonomic physiology: sympathetic and parasympathetic control

The autonomic nervous system regulates smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands, helping maintain internal stability without continuous conscious control. Sympathetic pathways generally arise from thoracolumbar spinal cord segments, while parasympathetic pathways arise from brainstem cranial nerves and sacral spinal cord segments. Both use preganglionic neurons that release acetylcholine at autonomic ganglia. Most parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine; most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release noradrenaline, with important exceptions such as sympathetic innervation of sweat glands. Responses depend on receptor type, organ and baseline state rather than a simple universal on/off switch.

High-yield study points

  • Sympathetic outflow is thoracolumbar; parasympathetic outflow is craniosacral.
  • Preganglionic autonomic neurons release acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors in autonomic ganglia.
  • Parasympathetic postganglionic neurons commonly release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors.
  • Sympathetic effects include context-dependent changes in heart rate, vascular tone, sweating and digestion.
  • Many organs receive dual innervation, but some structures are controlled mainly by one division.

Clinical connection

Autonomic dysfunction can affect blood pressure regulation, sweating, bowel and bladder function and heart-rate responses. Medication effects depend on receptor selectivity and the patient's context.

Autonomic organisation

The autonomic nervous system regulates cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, glands and visceral functions. Its main divisions are sympathetic and parasympathetic; the enteric nervous system also coordinates gastrointestinal activity and interacts with both. Most autonomic pathways use a two-neuron chain: a preganglionic neuron leaves the CNS and synapses in a peripheral autonomic ganglion with a postganglionic neuron that reaches the target organ.

Preganglionic sympathetic neurons arise from thoracolumbar spinal cord segments; parasympathetic outflow is craniosacral. All autonomic preganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors in ganglia. Most parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors; most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release noradrenaline onto adrenergic receptors. Important exceptions include sympathetic cholinergic fibres to many sweat glands.

Compare effects without oversimplifying

  • Sympathetic: generally supports mobilisation—raises cardiac activity, redirects blood flow, dilates pupils and reduces many digestive activities during acute demand.
  • Parasympathetic: generally supports maintenance—slows heart rate, promotes digestion and supports glandular secretion.
  • Enteric: local circuits coordinate motility, secretion and blood flow in the gut, with modulation by autonomic input.

These are tendencies, not a universal on/off switch. The effect depends on receptor subtype, organ, baseline tone and physiological context. Some organs receive dominant input from one division or have important local regulation.

Reflex arc and clinical logic

A visceral reflex includes a receptor, afferent pathway, central or peripheral integration, efferent autonomic pathway and effector. Baroreflexes rapidly adjust heart rate and vascular tone after blood pressure changes. Autonomic dysfunction can affect orthostatic blood pressure, sweating, bowel/bladder function and pupil responses; interpretation requires history and examination.

Self-test

  1. What neurotransmitter is released by all autonomic preganglionic neurons?
  2. Where does sympathetic outflow originate?
  3. Why are “sympathetic = always excitatory” and “parasympathetic = always inhibitory” incorrect?
Answers with reasoning

1. Acetylcholine. 2. Thoracolumbar spinal cord. 3. Effects depend on receptor subtype and target organ; each division can stimulate some functions and inhibit others.
