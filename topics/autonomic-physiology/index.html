Autonomic physiology: sympathetic and parasympathetic control

The autonomic nervous system regulates smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands, helping maintain internal stability without continuous conscious control. Sympathetic pathways generally arise from thoracolumbar spinal cord segments, while parasympathetic pathways arise from brainstem cranial nerves and sacral spinal cord segments. Both use preganglionic neurons that release acetylcholine at autonomic ganglia. Most parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine; most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release noradrenaline, with important exceptions such as sympathetic innervation of sweat glands. Responses depend on receptor type, organ and baseline state rather than a simple universal on/off switch.

Learning objectives

  • Compare sympathetic and parasympathetic organisation.
  • Describe autonomic neurotransmitters at a basic level.
  • Explain how autonomic control contributes to homeostasis.

High-yield study points

  • Sympathetic outflow is thoracolumbar; parasympathetic outflow is craniosacral.
  • Preganglionic autonomic neurons release acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors in autonomic ganglia.
  • Parasympathetic postganglionic neurons commonly release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors.
  • Sympathetic effects include context-dependent changes in heart rate, vascular tone, sweating and digestion.
  • Many organs receive dual innervation, but some structures are controlled mainly by one division.

Clinical connection

Autonomic dysfunction can affect blood pressure regulation, sweating, bowel and bladder function and heart-rate responses. Medication effects depend on receptor selectivity and the patient's context.
