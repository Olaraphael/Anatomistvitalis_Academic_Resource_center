Congenital anomalies, teratology and developmental mechanisms

24 min studyFree learning resourceIncludes memory aid

Lesson overview

Congenital anomalies may have genetic, chromosomal, environmental, multifactorial or unknown causes. Teratology studies how exposures and biological factors disturb development. Effects depend on timing, dose, duration, route, maternal-fetal factors and the process underway. Malformations arise from intrinsically abnormal formation; disruptions damage previously normal tissue; deformations result from mechanical forces; dysplasias reflect abnormal organisation of cells within a tissue. Conditions may be isolated or form a syndrome, sequence or association. Assessment can include imaging, family history, genetic testing and multidisciplinary counselling.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish malformation, disruption, deformation and dysplasia.
  • Explain stage-dependent vulnerability.
  • Communicate risk without making unsupported individual predictions.

High-yield points

  • Many structural malformations arise during organogenesis, while some tissues remain vulnerable later for growth or functional effects.
  • Not every congenital anomaly has an identifiable cause.
  • A syndrome is a recognisable pattern with a common cause; a sequence is a cascade from one primary event; an association is a non-random cluster whose cause may be uncertain.
  • Population-level risk does not determine an individual outcome.

Memory aid / mnemonic

M-D-D-D: Malformation = made wrong; Disruption = damage after formation; Deformation = force changes shape; Dysplasia = disordered tissue organisation.

Study tip: hide this section, explain the concept aloud, then check whether you remembered the sequence and its rationale.

Clinical connection

Avoid attributing congenital differences to an exposure without evidence. Suspected exposure or diagnosis calls for qualified obstetric, paediatric or genetic counselling.

Check your understanding

  1. What is a teratogen?
    Reveal answer

    An exposure or agent that can disrupt development and increase the risk of congenital anomalies, depending on timing, dose and other factors.

  2. Why does timing of exposure matter in embryonic development?
    Reveal answer

    Different structures form and mature at different times, so susceptibility and the type of potential effect vary with developmental stage.

  3. Does every congenital anomaly have an identifiable environmental cause?
    Reveal answer

    No. Causes may be genetic, environmental, multifactorial or unknown.
