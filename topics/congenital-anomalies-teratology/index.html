Congenital anomalies, teratology and developmental mechanisms

24 min studyFree learning resourceIncludes memory aid

Lesson overview

Congenital anomalies may have genetic, chromosomal, environmental, multifactorial or unknown causes. Teratology studies how exposures and biological factors disturb development. Effects depend on timing, dose, duration, route, maternal-fetal factors and the process underway. Malformations arise from intrinsically abnormal formation; disruptions damage previously normal tissue; deformations result from mechanical forces; dysplasias reflect abnormal organisation of cells within a tissue. Conditions may be isolated or form a syndrome, sequence or association. Assessment can include imaging, family history, genetic testing and multidisciplinary counselling.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish malformation, disruption, deformation and dysplasia.
  • Explain stage-dependent vulnerability.
  • Communicate risk without making unsupported individual predictions.

High-yield points

  • Many structural malformations arise during organogenesis, while some tissues remain vulnerable later for growth or functional effects.
  • Not every congenital anomaly has an identifiable cause.
  • A syndrome is a recognisable pattern with a common cause; a sequence is a cascade from one primary event; an association is a non-random cluster whose cause may be uncertain.
  • Population-level risk does not determine an individual outcome.

Memory aid / mnemonic

M-D-D-D: Malformation = made wrong; Disruption = damage after formation; Deformation = force changes shape; Dysplasia = disordered tissue organisation.

Study tip: hide this section, explain the concept aloud, then check whether you remembered the sequence and its rationale.

Clinical connection

Avoid attributing congenital differences to an exposure without evidence. Suspected exposure or diagnosis calls for qualified obstetric, paediatric or genetic counselling.

Check your understanding

  1. What is a teratogen?
    Reveal answer

    An exposure or agent that can disrupt development and increase the risk of congenital anomalies, depending on timing, dose and other factors.

  2. Why does timing of exposure matter in embryonic development?
    Reveal answer

    Different structures form and mature at different times, so susceptibility and the type of potential effect vary with developmental stage.

  3. Does every congenital anomaly have an identifiable environmental cause?
    Reveal answer

    No. Causes may be genetic, environmental, multifactorial or unknown.

Risk depends on the specific exposure, dose, timing, route, maternal and fetal factors, and evidence quality. Do not infer that an exposure caused an anomaly from temporal association alone; consult current specialist guidance for clinical decisions.

Classifying congenital anomalies

Precise classification helps connect a clinical finding to a developmental mechanism. Categories are useful but may overlap in complex cases.

  • Malformation: an intrinsic error in formation of a structure during development, such as an abnormality arising from altered tissue patterning or organ formation.
  • Disruption: breakdown or damage to a structure that was developing normally, for example due to a vascular or mechanical event.
  • Deformation: alteration in shape or position due to mechanical forces, often acting over time on a developing structure.
  • Dysplasia: abnormal organisation of cells within a tissue, which may affect multiple structures containing that tissue.
  • Sequence: a cascade of anomalies arising from one initiating defect; a syndrome is a recognisable pattern of findings associated with a common cause or mechanism.

How to assess a possible teratogenic exposure

  1. Identify the exact agent and whether the evidence concerns humans, animals or cell models.
  2. Establish dose, route, duration and timing relative to fertilisation and gestational age.
  3. Consider the developmental process and structures vulnerable at that time.
  4. Assess the strength of evidence, confounding factors, baseline risk and the possibility of chance association.
  5. Use current authoritative guidance for clinical counselling; avoid definitive claims based on a single report.

Critical distinction: hazard versus risk

A hazard is the potential of an agent to cause harm under some conditions; risk is the likelihood of harm under a particular exposure scenario. A substance that causes an effect at a high experimental dose does not automatically establish the same risk at every human exposure level. Conversely, absence of strong evidence is not always proof of no risk.

Ethical and clinical communication

Discuss uncertainty clearly, avoid blaming pregnant people for outcomes that may have multiple causes, and refer individual exposure questions to qualified clinicians or teratology information services. Surveillance data, case reports, animal experiments and controlled human studies answer different questions and have different limitations.
