Renal physiology: filtration, reabsorption and fluid balance

The kidneys regulate extracellular fluid volume, electrolytes, acid-base balance and waste excretion, and contribute to blood pressure regulation, erythropoiesis and vitamin D activation. Urine formation involves glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption and tubular secretion. These processes are selectively regulated along nephron segments rather than occurring uniformly throughout the kidney.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption, tubular secretion and excretion.
  • Describe the major transport roles of the proximal tubule, loop of Henle and distal nephron.
  • Explain how ADH, aldosterone and the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system influence fluid and electrolyte balance.
  • Summarise the kidney’s roles in acid–base regulation, blood pressure control and erythropoiesis.

High-yield study points

  • Glomerular filtration transfers water and small solutes from blood into the nephron while normally retaining cells and most plasma proteins.
  • Filtered substances may be reabsorbed from tubular fluid back to blood or secreted from blood into tubular fluid; excretion reflects filtration plus secretion minus reabsorption.
  • The proximal tubule reabsorbs a large proportion of filtered sodium, water, bicarbonate, glucose and amino acids under normal conditions.
  • The loop of Henle and associated medullary gradients support concentration or dilution of urine; the thick ascending limb reabsorbs salt but is relatively impermeable to water.
  • Antidiuretic hormone increases water permeability in collecting ducts; aldosterone promotes sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion in relevant distal nephron segments.
  • The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system contributes to regulation of blood pressure, sodium balance and extracellular fluid volume.
  • The kidneys participate in acid-base control by reclaiming filtered bicarbonate and excreting acid, including as ammonium and titratable acids.

Clinical connection

Changes in urine output, electrolytes or kidney function have many possible causes. Interpretation requires trends, medications, hydration status and laboratory context.

Check your understanding

  1. Write the relationship between excretion, filtration, reabsorption and secretion.
    Reveal answer

    Excretion = filtration − reabsorption + secretion.

  2. Why can the thick ascending limb help build a medullary osmotic gradient without directly allowing water to follow?
    Reveal answer

    It reabsorbs solutes but is relatively impermeable to water, helping separate salt transport from water movement.

  3. What is the principal collecting-duct effect of ADH?
    Reveal answer

    It increases water permeability, including through aquaporin-2 insertion in appropriate collecting-duct cells.

The nephron: four linked processes

At the glomerulus, filtration moves water and small solutes from plasma into Bowman’s space while normally retaining blood cells and most large proteins. Tubules selectively reabsorb substances back to blood and secrete selected substances into tubular fluid. A useful relationship is Excretion = filtration − reabsorption + secretion. The balance differs by substance.

Segmental logic

  • Proximal tubule: reabsorbs most filtered sodium and water, much bicarbonate, and nearly all filtered glucose and amino acids under normal conditions.
  • Loop of Henle: helps establish the medullary osmotic gradient; the thick ascending limb reabsorbs salts but is relatively impermeable to water.
  • Distal tubule and collecting system: fine-tune electrolyte and water handling under hormonal control.

Glomerular filtration depends on filtration pressures and the filtration coefficient. Renal autoregulation helps stabilise blood flow and filtration across a range of perfusion pressures.

Hormones and whole-body roles

ADH (vasopressin) increases collecting-duct water permeability through aquaporin-2 insertion. Aldosterone promotes sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion in appropriate distal nephron cells. The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system is activated by signals including reduced renal perfusion, reduced sodium chloride delivery to the macula densa and sympathetic stimulation.

The kidneys regulate acid–base balance by reclaiming filtered bicarbonate, secreting hydrogen ions and generating new bicarbonate. They also produce erythropoietin and activate vitamin D, linking renal function to red-cell production and mineral balance.
