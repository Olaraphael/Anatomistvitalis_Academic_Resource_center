Cardiovascular physiology: cardiac output, blood pressure and flow

The cardiovascular system transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones and waste products while helping regulate temperature, pH and tissue fluid balance. Cardiac output is the volume pumped by one ventricle per minute and equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Blood flow depends on pressure gradients and vascular resistance, while arterial pressure reflects cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance in a simplified steady-state relationship.

Learning objectives

  • Calculate cardiac output from heart rate and stroke volume and explain factors affecting stroke volume.
  • Describe the sequence of the cardiac cycle and relate valve events to pressure changes.
  • Distinguish electrical events on an ECG from mechanical contraction and relaxation.
  • Explain the simplified relationships among flow, pressure gradient, resistance and arterial pressure.

High-yield study points

  • Stroke volume is the volume ejected by a ventricle per beat; it is influenced by preload, contractility and afterload.
  • Cardiac output (CO) = heart rate (HR) × stroke volume (SV); units should be kept consistent, commonly litres per minute.
  • Mean arterial pressure is related approximately to cardiac output multiplied by systemic vascular resistance, with central venous pressure usually small in the simplified equation.
  • Resistance is strongly affected by vessel radius; small changes in arteriolar diameter can substantially alter flow and pressure.
  • Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch provide rapid feedback about arterial stretch and help adjust autonomic output.
  • Venous return depends on pressure gradients and is aided by skeletal-muscle pumps, respiratory pressure changes and venous valves in many regions.
  • Local metabolic factors, endothelial signals, autonomic activity and hormones all influence vascular tone; their effects differ by tissue.

Clinical connection

Blood pressure is one measurement in a larger system. Its interpretation depends on measurement technique, repeated readings, symptoms, age, medications and the clinical context.

Check your understanding

  1. A heart rate is 75 beats/min and stroke volume is 70 mL/beat. What is cardiac output?
    Reveal answer

    75 × 70 = 5,250 mL/min, or 5.25 L/min.

  2. Does the QRS complex directly represent ventricular contraction?
    Reveal answer

    No. It represents ventricular depolarisation, which normally precedes mechanical contraction.

  3. What happens to flow if the pressure gradient rises while resistance stays constant?
    Reveal answer

    Flow rises in proportion to the pressure gradient in the simplified relationship Flow = pressure difference ÷ resistance.

Build the cardiovascular model

Blood moves because a pressure gradient drives flow through resistance. In simplified form, Flow = pressure difference ÷ resistance. Cardiac output equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Stroke volume depends on preload (ventricular filling), contractility (strength at a given load) and afterload (the load opposing ejection). Arterioles are major adjustable resistance vessels; veins are highly compliant capacitance vessels.

Cardiac cycle in order

  1. Ventricular filling occurs when atrioventricular valves are open.
  2. Atrial systole tops up ventricular filling.
  3. Isovolumetric contraction begins after ventricular depolarisation; all valves are closed and pressure rises.
  4. When ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure, semilunar valves open and ejection occurs.
  5. Ventricular relaxation begins; all valves are briefly closed during isovolumetric relaxation.
  6. When ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, atrioventricular valves open and filling resumes.

Electrical activity and blood pressure

The sinoatrial node normally initiates the heartbeat. Conduction through the atrioventricular node delays the impulse before the His–Purkinje system rapidly activates the ventricles. ECG P wave = atrial depolarisation; QRS complex = ventricular depolarisation; T wave = ventricular repolarisation. These are electrical events, not direct recordings of contraction or valve movement.

Arterial pressure is influenced by cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance. Carotid sinus and aortic arch baroreceptors detect stretch and rapidly alter autonomic output. Renal sodium/water handling and hormonal systems contribute to longer-term control.

Self-test

  1. Write the cardiac output equation.
  2. What happens during isovolumetric contraction?
  3. What does the QRS complex represent?
Answers with reasoning

1. Heart rate × stroke volume. 2. Ventricular pressure rises while all valves are closed. 3. Ventricular depolarisation.
