Perineum: triangles, pouches, external genitalia and pudendal nerve

An in-depth guide to perineal boundaries, anal and urogenital triangles, fascial spaces, perineal muscles, erectile tissues and neurovascular pathways.

Return to pelvis & perineum courseBrowse all topics

1. Boundaries and triangles

The perineum is the diamond-shaped region inferior to the pelvic diaphragm, between the thighs. Its anterior point is the pubic symphysis, posterolateral points are the ischial tuberosities, and posterior point is the coccyx; the sacrotuberous ligaments contribute to its posterolateral boundary. A line joining the ischial tuberosities divides it into the anterior urogenital triangle and posterior anal triangle. The perineum includes external genital structures and the anal opening, but the pelvic cavity and pelvic floor lie superior to it.

Anal triangle

The anal triangle contains the anal canal and external anal sphincter, with ischioanal fossae on either side. Each ischioanal fossa is a fat-filled space allowing expansion of the anal canal during defaecation. Its boundaries include the levator ani medially, obturator internus and its fascia laterally, and posterior structures associated with gluteus maximus and sacrotuberous ligament. The pudendal canal lies in obturator fascia on the lateral wall and transmits the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels. Infection can spread through these spaces; the pattern depends on fascial boundaries and the source.

Urogenital triangle

The urogenital triangle contains the external urethral opening and external genital structures. The perineal membrane provides an important deep boundary for the anterior perineum. Superficial perineal muscles include ischiocavernosus, bulbospongiosus and superficial transverse perineal muscle. Their functions include stabilising erectile tissue roots, assisting aspects of erection and ejaculation, and supporting the perineal body; exact actions differ by sex and context.

2. Pouches and erectile tissues

The superficial perineal pouch lies between the perineal membrane and superficial perineal fascia and contains erectile tissue roots and superficial muscles. The deep perineal region is associated with the external urethral sphincter complex and other structures; textbook descriptions differ in how strictly they define a “deep perineal pouch”, particularly in the female pelvis. Learn the relationships and fascial boundaries rather than memorising a list without spatial context.

In males, the root of the penis includes the bulb and crura; the bulb is associated with bulbospongiosus and the crura with ischiocavernosus. In females, the vestibular bulbs and crura of the clitoris occupy related but not identical positions. The greater vestibular glands are posterolateral to the vaginal opening; their ducts open into the vestibule. Anatomical terminology should be precise and respectful, and clinical examination requires informed consent and appropriate professional practice.

3. Pudendal nerve route and branches

The pudendal nerve arises mainly from S2–S4. It leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, passes near the ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament, then enters the perineum through the lesser sciatic foramen. It runs in the pudendal canal with the internal pudendal vessels. Its major terminal branches include the inferior rectal nerves, perineal nerve and dorsal nerve of the penis or clitoris. Branching patterns and communications vary.

The inferior rectal branches supply the external anal sphincter and perianal skin. The perineal nerve supplies muscles and skin of the perineum and contributes to posterior scrotal or labial sensation. The dorsal nerve of the penis/clitoris carries important somatic sensory information. Pudendal innervation does not explain every visceral pelvic sensation: autonomic and visceral afferent pathways also contribute.

4. Vessels and applied anatomy

The internal pudendal artery is a major arterial supply to the perineum. It follows a route similar to the pudendal nerve and gives branches including inferior rectal, perineal and dorsal/deep genital branches, with variation. The ischioanal fossa contains fat and allows expansion but can also provide a route for spread of infection. Pudendal nerve block uses landmarks near the ischial spine, but it is a clinical procedure requiring trained personnel, anatomical knowledge and appropriate safety protocols.

Clinical connections

  • Perineal tears during childbirth vary in depth; injuries involving the anal sphincter complex require accurate clinical assessment and management.
  • Pudendal neuropathy may cause perineal pain or sensory disturbance, but similar symptoms have many causes and require evaluation.
  • Ischioanal abscesses may present with severe pain, swelling or fever and need urgent clinical care.
  • New saddle numbness with bladder/bowel dysfunction or leg weakness is a red flag for serious nerve compression and requires emergency assessment.

Self-test

  1. What divides the perineum into two triangles?
    Answer

    An imaginary line joining the ischial tuberosities.

  2. What route does the pudendal nerve take around the ischial spine?
    Answer

    It exits via the greater sciatic foramen, passes near the ischial spine/sacrospinous ligament, then enters via the lesser sciatic foramen.

  3. What is the role of the ischioanal fossa?
    Answer

    Its fat permits expansion of the anal canal and it forms a clinically relevant space for infection spread.

Study and safety note

Perineum: fascial spaces, muscle layers and neurovascular pathways

Surface boundaries and deep organisation

The perineum is a diamond-shaped region inferior to the pelvic diaphragm. The pubic symphysis forms its anterior point, the coccyx its posterior point, and the ischial tuberosities its lateral reference points; the sacrotuberous ligaments contribute to the posterolateral boundaries. A line between the ischial tuberosities divides the urogenital triangle anteriorly from the anal triangle posteriorly. The perineal membrane spans the anterior triangle and provides attachment for muscles and support for structures passing through it. It should not be confused with the pelvic diaphragm, which lies superiorly and forms the principal muscular floor of the pelvic cavity.

Anal triangle and ischioanal fossae

The anal triangle contains the anal canal, external anal sphincter and paired ischioanal fossae. Each fossa contains fat that permits expansion of the anal canal during defaecation. Its medial boundary is formed by the external anal sphincter and levator ani; laterally lie obturator internus and its fascia; posteriorly are structures associated with gluteus maximus and the sacrotuberous ligament. The pudendal canal is formed in obturator fascia on the lateral wall and transmits the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels. Infection may spread through the fossa or across posterior/deep communications depending on the involved fascial plane.

Urogenital triangle and perineal muscles

Superficial perineal muscles include ischiocavernosus, bulbospongiosus and superficial transverse perineal muscles. They relate to erectile tissues and the perineal body and can assist with stabilisation or expulsion functions. Deeper structures include the external urethral sphincter complex and associated compressor or urethrovaginal components where present. The arrangement differs between sexes, and modern descriptions recognise a complex external urethral sphincter rather than treating the region as one identical sheet in all bodies. The perineal membrane provides a key spatial reference for the superficial and deep regions.

Pudendal nerve: course and branches

The pudendal nerve arises from S2–S4 anterior rami. It leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, passes close to the ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament, and enters the perineum through the lesser sciatic foramen. It travels with internal pudendal vessels in the pudendal canal and commonly divides into inferior rectal, perineal and dorsal nerve of the penis or clitoris branches. The inferior rectal nerve supplies the external anal sphincter and perianal skin; perineal branches supply perineal muscles and sensory territory; the dorsal nerve carries sensation from the external genital region. Branching patterns and communications vary.

Pudendal nerve entrapment is one possible cause of perineal pain, but the diagnosis is clinical and other causes must be excluded. A nerve block is performed by trained clinicians using anatomical landmarks and appropriate safety precautions; anatomical knowledge alone is not sufficient to perform a procedure.

Applied relationships

  • Perineal body injury may affect support and continence because several muscles and connective-tissue fibres converge there.
  • Injury to the external anal sphincter or its nerve supply can impair voluntary continence.
  • Urethral injury may be associated with pelvic trauma; suspected injury requires urgent clinical assessment.
  • The ischioanal fossae and fascial planes explain pathways of local infection, but imaging and examination determine the actual extent.

Perineal anatomy in depth: fascia, spaces, muscles and innervation

Boundaries and layered anatomy

The perineum is a diamond-shaped region inferior to the pelvic diaphragm. Its anterior point is the pubic symphysis, posterior point the coccyx and lateral landmarks the ischial tuberosities; the sacrotuberous ligaments contribute to the posterolateral boundary. A line joining the ischial tuberosities separates the anterior urogenital triangle from the posterior anal triangle. The perineal membrane is a strong fascial sheet in the anterior triangle and provides attachment for muscles and support for structures passing through it. It is not the same structure as the pelvic diaphragm, which lies superiorly.

Ischioanal fossae and anal canal

The ischioanal fossae are paired fat-filled spaces lateral to the anal canal. The fat allows the anal canal to expand during defaecation. The medial boundary includes the external anal sphincter and levator ani; the lateral wall is associated with obturator internus and its fascia. The pudendal canal lies in the obturator fascia on the lateral wall and contains the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels. Infection can spread through these spaces and may cross posterior or deep communications, depending on its origin and fascial boundaries.

Superficial and deep perineal structures

Superficial perineal muscles include ischiocavernosus, bulbospongiosus and superficial transverse perineal muscle. Ischiocavernosus surrounds the crura of erectile tissue; bulbospongiosus surrounds the bulb or vestibular region and contributes to functions that vary by sex; transverse perineal muscles help stabilise the perineal body. The external urethral sphincter complex is part of the continence mechanism and has sex-related anatomical differences. Descriptions of a discrete “deep perineal pouch” differ between textbooks, especially for the female pelvis; understand the fascia and relationships rather than treating all diagrams as identical.

Perineal body and childbirth

The perineal body is a central fibromuscular node between the urogenital structures and anal canal. Several muscles and connective-tissue fibres converge here, including components associated with bulbospongiosus, transverse perineal muscles and the external anal sphincter complex. Injury can compromise support and continence depending on depth and structures involved. Obstetric perineal tears are classified clinically by the structures affected; injuries involving the anal sphincter complex require accurate recognition and appropriate care.

Pudendal nerve: route and territories

The pudendal nerve arises mainly from S2–S4. It leaves through the greater sciatic foramen, passes near the ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament, and re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen before travelling in the pudendal canal. Its principal branches include inferior rectal nerves, the perineal nerve and the dorsal nerve of the penis or clitoris. It supplies major somatic sensory territories of the perineum and motor supply to external sphincter and perineal muscles. Visceral pelvic pain and autonomic functions involve additional pathways, so not every pelvic symptom is a pudendal neuropathy.

Clinical localisation and safety

Pudendal neuropathy may be associated with perineal pain or altered sensation, but diagnosis requires careful clinical assessment and exclusion of other causes. Ischioanal abscess can produce severe pain, swelling and fever. New saddle numbness with bladder or bowel dysfunction, leg weakness or loss of perineal sensation can signal serious nerve compression and requires emergency assessment. Pudendal nerve block and other procedures are performed by trained clinicians, not from textbook landmarks alone.
