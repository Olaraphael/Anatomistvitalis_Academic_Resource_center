Research Methodology: From First-Degree Project to PhD Thesis

A step-by-step learning guide for students who need to develop a researchable question, choose a sound method, collect and analyse evidence, write clearly, reference accurately and defend their work honestly.

Written by Ibine Oladipupo R. · Anatomistvitalis Academic Resource Center

Important: This guide teaches general academic principles. Your department, university handbook, supervisor, ethics committee and funder may specify different formats or requirements. Their current instructions take precedence. A good project is not defined by page count alone; it is defined by a clear question, appropriate methods, reliable evidence, ethical conduct and transparent reporting.

1. What research methodology means

Research is a systematic process for answering a question using evidence. Methodology is the reasoning behind the approach: why a particular design, population, measurement and analysis are suitable for the question. Methods are the specific procedures used to recruit participants or select specimens, collect observations, measure variables and analyse data. The methodology chapter should explain both what was done and why those decisions were appropriate.

Research may be quantitative (numerical measurement and statistical analysis), qualitative (meaning, experience, language or social processes), or mixed-methods (a planned integration of both). Laboratory, anatomical, clinical, observational, experimental, educational and documentary studies each have different requirements. Do not select a design simply because it is familiar; select one that can answer the question safely and credibly.

2. Choose a topic and formulate a research question

Start with a broad area of interest, then narrow it to a specific, feasible and answerable problem. A topic such as “diabetes” is too broad for most student projects. A more focused question identifies the population or material, the factor or phenomenon of interest, the outcome, and—where relevant—the setting and period. Search recent reviews and primary studies before finalising the question; this helps establish what is known, what remains uncertain and whether the proposed work is worthwhile.

Check a proposed topic against these questions

  • Is the problem clearly stated and supported by credible literature?
  • Is there a genuine knowledge gap, local need, methodological question or replication rationale?
  • Can the question be answered with the available time, skills, equipment, data and budget?
  • Can the study be conducted ethically and with appropriate permissions?
  • Are the key concepts, exposure/intervention and outcomes defined in measurable terms?
  • Will the result matter to a scientific, clinical, educational or policy audience?

For quantitative studies, a focused question often leads to a primary objective and a testable hypothesis. A null hypothesis commonly states that no specified difference or association exists; an alternative hypothesis states the effect or relationship the study is designed to investigate. Qualitative studies may instead use open research questions and a clearly justified interpretive approach. Do not force a hypothesis into every design.

3. Review the literature systematically

A literature review is an analytical account of what reliable sources have established, where findings agree or conflict, which methods have been used, and what remains uncertain. It is not a sequence of unrelated summaries. Search scholarly databases and library catalogues using combinations of keywords, synonyms and subject headings. Depending on the discipline, useful starting points may include PubMed, Google Scholar, Cochrane Library, Scopus or Web of Science through institutional access, and discipline-specific databases.

Record the search date, databases, search terms, date limits and selection reasons in a reproducible way appropriate to the review type. Read the original paper rather than relying solely on its abstract. Evaluate the study design, sample, measurement validity, risk of bias, analysis, limitations and relevance to your own question. Prefer authoritative guidelines and original research when appropriate; use textbooks for foundational knowledge and recent reviews to orient yourself. Do not cite a paper you have not checked.

Keep a literature matrix with columns for author/year, research question, design, sample, measures, key findings, limitations and relevance. Organise the review by themes, concepts, methods or chronology only when chronology genuinely helps. End with a precise statement of the gap and explain how your study addresses it.

4. Choose and justify the research design

The design is the overall plan linking the research question to evidence. Common quantitative designs include cross-sectional studies, case-control studies, cohort studies, randomised experiments and laboratory experiments. Qualitative designs include interviews, focus groups, ethnography, phenomenology, grounded theory and qualitative case studies. Mixed-methods designs specify how quantitative and qualitative strands will be connected. Document analysis, systematic reviews and anatomical or histological studies have their own design-specific standards.

In a methods chapter, name the design, define its setting and time frame, explain why it fits the objective, and state its strengths and limitations. Observational association does not automatically demonstrate causation. Experimental work requires a defensible comparison, appropriate controls, prespecified outcomes and procedures that reduce bias where possible. For animal work, follow relevant institutional and national requirements and the principles of replacement, reduction and refinement.

5. Write a research proposal before collecting data

A proposal is a plan for work that has not yet been conducted. It should use future tense for planned procedures and must not present invented results as if they already exist. A common structure is:

  1. Title: concise, specific and consistent with the actual study.
  2. Background: introduce the topic and establish why the problem matters.
  3. Problem statement: define the particular uncertainty or need the study addresses.
  4. Rationale/significance: explain who may benefit and how the findings could contribute.
  5. Aim and objectives: state one overall aim and a manageable set of specific, measurable objectives.
  6. Research questions/hypotheses: align them with the objectives and design.
  7. Literature review: synthesise the most relevant evidence and establish the gap.
  8. Methods: design, setting, population/material, eligibility, sample size, sampling, variables, instruments/protocol, quality control, analysis and limitations.
  9. Ethical considerations: approvals, consent where relevant, confidentiality, risk management and humane treatment of animals where applicable.
  10. Work plan and budget: realistic stages and justified costs, when required.
  11. References and appendices: use the required style and attach relevant tools or permission documents.

6. Ethics, permissions and participant/specimen protection

Obtain required ethics approval and institutional permissions before starting regulated data collection, recruiting participants, accessing confidential records or beginning animal experiments. Approval cannot be retrospectively invented. The application should describe risks, benefits, recruitment, consent, privacy, data storage, adverse-event handling, conflicts of interest and plans for sharing results. Human-participant research requires appropriate consent or a formally approved waiver where applicable. Animal research requires trained personnel, justified animal numbers, humane procedures, suitable anaesthesia/analgesia and approved endpoints.

Never fabricate an approval number, consent, specimen provenance, participant count or experiment. If an approval detail is missing from an existing report, contact the responsible institution and report the situation transparently; do not make up a code. De-identify sensitive data, limit access, store files securely and follow local law and institutional policy. Ethical approval, informed consent and data protection are related but distinct obligations.

7. Define the population, sample and sampling method

The target population is the wider group to which the question relates; the study population is the accessible group meeting the study conditions; the sample is the subset actually studied. State inclusion and exclusion criteria clearly. Probability sampling methods include simple random, systematic, stratified and cluster sampling. Non-probability methods include convenience, purposive and snowball sampling; they may be suitable for some questions but impose limits on generalisation.

Sample size should be justified using the study design, primary outcome, expected variability or event rate, desired precision or power, significance level where relevant, anticipated non-response and practical constraints. Use an appropriate calculation or defensible qualitative rationale, and cite the assumptions. Do not choose a sample size merely because it is easy. For laboratory and animal experiments, identify the experimental unit, independent biological replicates, technical replicates, allocation, controls and any randomisation or blinding. Multiple measurements from one animal or person are not automatically independent samples.

8. Data collection, instruments and quality assurance

Describe exactly how each variable or observation will be obtained. Instruments may include questionnaires, interview guides, case-report forms, laboratory assays, anatomical measurements, histological scoring systems or validated clinical scales. Identify the source and version of an instrument, its validity and reliability evidence, any permissions, and how it will be scored. Pilot-test tools when appropriate and explain what the pilot changed.

For laboratory work, provide enough detail for another trained researcher to understand and reproduce the protocol: materials, reagents and concentrations, equipment, specimen handling, timing, temperature, controls, calibration, processing, staining, imaging and scoring rules as relevant. Report deviations rather than hiding them. Maintain a dated laboratory notebook or equivalent audit trail. Separate planned procedures from what was actually done.

9. Data management and analysis

Prepare a data dictionary before analysis. Define variable names, units, coding, allowable values, missing-value codes and derivations. Check entries for impossible values, duplicates, inconsistent units and missingness. Preserve a read-only raw-data copy and document cleaning decisions. Back up files securely and avoid storing identifiable data in public repositories.

Describe analysis methods in relation to each objective. Descriptive statistics summarise data: frequencies and percentages for categorical variables; means and standard deviations for reasonably symmetric continuous data; medians and interquartile ranges for skewed data. Inferential methods depend on the question, design, assumptions, sample and outcome. Report effect estimates and confidence intervals where appropriate, not only p-values. A p-value does not measure the size or practical importance of an effect, and statistical significance is not proof of causation.

For qualitative research, explain how recordings or notes are transcribed, how coding and themes are developed, who performs the analysis, how reflexivity and credibility are addressed, and how quotations are selected without exposing identities. For mixed methods, explain where the strands are integrated and what additional insight integration provides. State the software and version used when relevant, but remember that software does not replace a justified analytic plan.

10. Common structure of a completed research report

Follow your university's prescribed format first. A common biomedical dissertation structure is:

  1. Preliminary pages: title page, declaration/certification as required, dedication and acknowledgements if permitted, abstract, table of contents, lists of tables/figures and abbreviations.
  2. Chapter 1 — Introduction: background, problem statement, rationale/significance, aim, objectives, research questions or hypotheses, and scope or operational definitions where required.
  3. Chapter 2 — Literature review: critical synthesis of foundational and current evidence, theoretical/conceptual framework where relevant, and the identified gap.
  4. Chapter 3 — Materials and methods: design, setting, population/material, sampling, sample-size rationale, procedures, measures, quality control, analysis and ethics.
  5. Chapter 4 — Results: findings presented in an order that follows the objectives, with clear tables and figures.
  6. Chapter 5 — Discussion, conclusion and recommendations: interpretation, comparison with literature, implications, strengths, limitations, conclusions supported by the data and proportionate recommendations.
  7. End matter: references and appendices, including instruments or supplementary materials as required.

Some departments require a combined results-and-discussion chapter, a separate conclusion chapter, or a different chapter sequence. A thesis-by-publication may follow another format. Use the approved handbook, not a generic website, as the final authority.

11. How to write a first-degree / undergraduate project

An undergraduate project demonstrates that the student can define a manageable problem, understand relevant literature, follow a suitable method, handle evidence responsibly and communicate findings. The scope is usually narrower than a master's dissertation or PhD thesis. A modest, well-executed question is better than an ambitious project that cannot be completed reliably.

Practical workflow

  1. Read the departmental project handbook and confirm the required format, deadlines and assessment criteria.
  2. Discuss a focused topic with a supervisor; agree on the question, objectives, study design and feasibility.
  3. Conduct a targeted literature search and build a reference library as you read.
  4. Draft the proposal and methods in enough detail for review; obtain the required approvals before data collection.
  5. Collect and record data using the approved protocol. Keep original records and document any deviation.
  6. Clean and analyse the data according to the objectives. Seek statistical advice before analysis if the design is unfamiliar.
  7. Write each chapter, revise for logic and clarity, and check that tables, figures and claims match the original data.
  8. Verify every citation, run the department's required similarity/integrity checks, submit in the prescribed format and prepare for the oral presentation.

Do not copy a previous student's project and replace a few words. A previous project may help you understand format, but its text, data, images and references remain subject to authorship and permission rules. Never invent results to fill a chapter. If results are inconclusive or no effect is observed, report them accurately and discuss what the design can and cannot establish.

12. How a master's dissertation differs

A master's dissertation generally expects greater independence, a deeper and more critical literature review, a stronger justification of methodological choices, more sophisticated analysis and a clearer contribution than an undergraduate project. The contribution may be contextual, methodological, empirical or applied; it does not necessarily have to transform an entire field. Expectations vary by taught master's, research master's and discipline.

Students should demonstrate command of competing explanations, assess the quality of evidence, justify sample and analysis decisions, acknowledge limitations, and explain the implications without overstating generalisability. Maintain a documented timeline with supervisor feedback milestones. Agree early on authorship, data ownership, access permissions, publication plans and the dissertation format. Where appropriate, preregister the protocol or analysis plan before observing outcomes.

13. How a PhD thesis differs

A doctoral thesis is expected to make an original, defensible contribution to knowledge and demonstrate that the candidate can conduct independent research at an advanced level. The contribution might be a new finding, theory, method, dataset, critical synthesis or a meaningful extension or replication that changes understanding. Novelty should be demonstrated through a careful review of the field, not simply asserted.

A PhD usually requires a coherent programme of work, explicit conceptual or theoretical positioning where relevant, rigorous methodological justification, transparent reporting, critical interpretation and a clear statement of original contribution. The candidate should explain why the work matters, how it changes or strengthens existing knowledge, what remains uncertain and what future research is warranted. A thesis may be monograph-based or publication-based depending on institutional regulations.

Doctoral candidates should agree on milestones and supervisory responsibilities, keep auditable records, manage research risks, document protocol amendments, preserve data and code where possible, and plan for reproducibility and dissemination. A large volume of pages or many publications does not automatically constitute a doctoral contribution. The institution's regulations define examination, thesis format, embargo, publication and deposit requirements.

14. Academic writing and referencing

Write for a reader who needs to understand the argument and reproduce the work. Use precise terms, define abbreviations at first use, maintain consistent tense, and make each paragraph serve one purpose. The introduction explains why the question matters; methods explain what was done; results report what was found; discussion explains what the findings mean in relation to existing evidence. Avoid inflated language and unsupported claims.

Use the citation style required by the department—such as APA, Vancouver, Harvard or another approved style—and apply it consistently. A reference manager such as Zotero can help organise sources, but automatically generated citations must still be checked against the original source and the required style. Cite the source of ideas, data, methods, adapted tables and images. Direct quotations should be rare in scientific writing and clearly marked. A reference list must include only sources actually cited, and every in-text citation must have a matching reference.

Plagiarism includes presenting another person's words, ideas, data, code or visual material as one's own. Paraphrasing requires genuine restatement and citation; changing a few words is not enough. Disclose permitted use of AI tools when required by institutional policy, verify all generated claims and references, and never use AI to fabricate data, ethics approvals, citations or research activity.

15. Results, discussion, conclusions and recommendations

Present results in the order of the objectives or research questions. Give each table or figure a clear number, descriptive title, units and notes explaining abbreviations or statistical symbols. Refer to each item in the text and avoid repeating every number in both the table and prose. Do not hide unfavourable, unexpected or non-significant findings. Distinguish prespecified analyses from exploratory analyses.

In the discussion, state the principal finding, interpret it cautiously, compare it with relevant studies, explain plausible reasons for agreement or disagreement, and consider alternative explanations and bias. Address strengths and limitations, including measurement error, confounding, sample limitations, missing data and external validity as relevant. A conclusion should answer the research question and stay within what the data support. Recommendations should follow from the findings and be realistic; future studies should address clearly identified uncertainties.

16. Preparing for presentation or viva voce defence

Build a short presentation around the problem, gap, aim, methods, principal findings, interpretation, limitations and contribution. Use readable slides and figures that you are authorised to show. Practise explaining the design and why it was appropriate, how the sample was chosen, how variables were measured, what the analysis means, and what the study cannot conclude. Prepare to acknowledge limitations openly. If you do not know an answer, explain your reasoning and identify what evidence would resolve the uncertainty rather than guessing.

17. Research integrity and common avoidable errors

  • Changing objectives after seeing results without disclosing the change.
  • Fabricating or altering observations, images, measurements, citations, sample sizes or approval details.
  • Using statistical tests without checking whether their assumptions and data structure are appropriate.
  • Claiming causation from an observational association without a valid causal basis.
  • Writing a literature review as a list of summaries rather than a critical synthesis.
  • Using unattributed images, copied tables or unlicensed material.
  • Reporting only results that support the preferred conclusion.
  • Ignoring contradictory findings, limitations, conflicts of interest or relevant protocol deviations.
  • Leaving referencing until the final day, when missing source details are difficult to recover.

18. Final submission checklist

  • ☐ The title, question, aim, objectives, methods and conclusions agree with one another.
  • ☐ The format follows the current departmental handbook.
  • ☐ Required ethical approvals and permissions were obtained and are reported accurately.
  • ☐ The design, sample, instruments, procedures and analysis are described sufficiently.
  • ☐ Results reflect the actual dataset; all tables and figures have been checked.
  • ☐ Limitations and unexpected findings are discussed honestly.
  • ☐ Every citation is verified and every reference is matched to the text.
  • ☐ Permissions and attribution for third-party figures, scales and data are in place.
  • ☐ Appendices, declarations, pagination and formatting meet institutional requirements.
  • ☐ Data and research records are stored securely according to policy.
  • ☐ The work has been proofread for logic, grammar, consistency and accessibility.

19. Reporting and learning resources

Choose reporting guidance that matches the study design; these are not interchangeable checklists and do not replace a university's rules.

  • EQUATOR Network — reporting guidelines for many health research designs, including CONSORT, STROBE, PRISMA, CARE and others.
  • CONSORT — reporting randomised trials.
  • STROBE — reporting observational studies.
  • PRISMA — reporting systematic reviews and meta-analyses.
  • PubMed Central — a free full-text archive of biomedical and life-sciences literature.
  • Zotero — a reference-management tool; always verify exported citations.
Last principle: Plan carefully, record what actually happened, analyse data with methods suited to the question, cite sources honestly, and report limitations transparently. These habits matter at every academic level—from a first-degree project to a doctoral thesis.
