Core study guides for Anatomy & Physiology

Build a connected understanding of how the human body develops, how the nervous system is organised, and how organs maintain life. These guides move from first principles to clinical reasoning without assuming that you already know the subject.

How to use these guides

For each topic, first explain the process in your own words, then sketch or label it from memory, and finally answer a question that asks you to predict what happens when one step fails. Do not treat a list of facts as understanding until you can explain the relationship between them.

Study cycle

Learn → draw → retrieve → apply

  1. Read one short section for meaning.
  2. Draw the sequence or structure without looking.
  3. Check and correct your drawing.
  4. Answer a question and explain why the alternatives are wrong.
A reliable framework

Ask four questions

What is it? Define it. Where is it? Locate it. How does it work or develop? Explain the mechanism. Why does it matter? Connect it to function, clinical findings or a developmental outcome.

Guide 01 · Development

Embryology: from fertilisation to a functioning body

Embryology explains how one fertilised cell produces organised tissues, organs and body regions. Learn the timeline and the mechanism together; the timeline tells you when, while the mechanism explains how.

The developmental sequence

1

Gametogenesis and fertilisation

Meiosis produces haploid gametes. At fertilisation, sperm and oocyte membranes interact, the oocyte completes meiosis II, and the parental genomes combine to establish a diploid zygote. Cleavage begins without a substantial increase in the overall size of the conceptus.

2

Cleavage, morula and blastocyst

Repeated mitoses produce blastomeres, then a compact morula. Fluid enters to form the blastocyst, whose trophoblast contributes to fetal portions of the placenta and whose embryoblast forms the embryo proper. Implantation begins around the end of the first week, with timing varying slightly.

3

Bilaminar disc and gastrulation

The embryoblast forms epiblast and hypoblast. During gastrulation, epiblast cells migrate through the primitive streak: some replace the hypoblast to form definitive endoderm, others form intraembryonic mesoderm, and remaining epiblast becomes ectoderm. This establishes the three primary germ layers and the body axes.

4

Neurulation and body folding

Ectoderm over the notochord thickens into the neural plate, which folds and closes as the neural tube. Neural crest cells detach and migrate to form many peripheral nervous system structures and other derivatives. Head-to-tail and lateral folding help convert a flat disc into a three-dimensional body and incorporate part of the yolk sac into the gut tube.

5

Organogenesis, placenta and fetal period

During weeks 4–8, organ primordia are established and the embryo is particularly vulnerable to disruptions that cause major structural anomalies. From week 9 to birth, growth and functional maturation become prominent. The placenta supports exchange and endocrine functions; maternal and fetal blood normally remain in separate circulations across the placental barrier.

Dating matters: gestational age counted from the last menstrual period is usually about two weeks greater than age counted from fertilisation. State which convention you are using in an examination answer.

Germ-layer logic

Ectoderm: epidermis and nervous system, including neural tube-derived central nervous system.

Mesoderm: muscle, connective tissues, bone, cardiovascular structures, kidneys and much of the urogenital system.

Endoderm: epithelial lining of much of the gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts and associated glandular parenchyma, including liver and pancreas.

These are core patterns, not exhaustive lists; each organ also depends on interactions between tissues.

Think in cause and effect

Congenital abnormalities can follow altered cell proliferation, migration, fusion, signalling, tissue separation or programmed cell death. A useful answer identifies the normal step first, the disrupted step second, and the resulting anatomical consequence third.

Example: failure of neural tube closure can result in neural tube defects. Risk is influenced by multiple factors, including folate status, genetics and certain exposures.

Embryology self-check

Why is gastrulation a decisive event?

It establishes the three germ layers and contributes to body-axis organisation. These layers then interact and differentiate to generate the body's tissues and organs.

What is the difference between the neural tube and neural crest?

The neural tube forms the brain and spinal cord. Neural crest cells arise at the neural plate border and migrate widely, contributing to many peripheral ganglia, Schwann cells, melanocytes and craniofacial structures, among other derivatives.

Why is the embryonic period especially sensitive to teratogens?

Major organ systems are being established through coordinated growth, migration and tissue interactions. Interference during a critical developmental window can alter structural formation; effects depend on timing, dose, mechanism and susceptibility.

Guide 02 · Nervous system

Neuroanatomy: map the structure before memorising pathways

Neuroanatomy becomes easier when every structure is located in a three-dimensional map and every pathway is learned by its origin, relay, crossing point, destination and function.

1. The central map

The central nervous system (CNS) consists of brain and spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) includes cranial and spinal nerves, ganglia and peripheral receptors. The somatic system carries sensory information from body walls and supports skeletal muscle control; the autonomic system regulates smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands.

In the brain, distinguish cerebral hemispheres, diencephalon, brainstem (midbrain, pons and medulla) and cerebellum. In the spinal cord, identify grey matter (neuronal cell bodies and local circuitry) and white matter (myelinated and unmyelinated axons organised into tracts).

2. Grey matter, white matter and support

A nucleus is a collection of neuronal cell bodies within the CNS; a ganglion is a collection in the PNS. A tract is a bundle of CNS axons; a nerve is a bundle of PNS axons. These naming conventions are important but should be understood as anatomical patterns rather than isolated vocabulary.

Astrocytes regulate the neural environment and support synapses; oligodendrocytes myelinate CNS axons; Schwann cells myelinate PNS axons; microglia are resident immune cells; ependymal cells line the ventricular system and central canal.

3. Learn a pathway with five checkpoints

  1. Modality: what information or command is carried?
  2. First neuron: where is its cell body?
  3. Relay: where does the next synapse occur?
  4. Decussation: where, if anywhere, do fibres cross?
  5. Destination: where does the signal terminate and what does it do?

4. Three high-yield pathways

Dorsal column–medial lemniscus: fine touch, vibration and conscious proprioception; first-order fibres ascend ipsilaterally in the spinal cord, synapse in the medulla, cross, and continue to the thalamus and somatosensory cortex.

Spinothalamic system: pain, temperature and aspects of crude touch; fibres usually synapse soon after entering the spinal cord and second-order axons cross through the anterior white commissure, then ascend contralaterally.

Corticospinal tract: voluntary skilled movement; most fibres cross in the caudal medulla at the pyramidal decussation and descend in the lateral corticospinal tract.

Clinical localisation rule: determine whether a finding is sensory, motor, cranial-nerve, cerebellar or autonomic; establish its side; then use the known crossing point and level of the pathway to localise the lesion. Real patients may have mixed findings, so localisation must use the complete examination.

Neuroanatomy self-check

A patient loses vibration sense in the right leg from a spinal cord lesion. Which side of the cord is most likely affected?

A right-sided dorsal column lesion below the medullary crossing can impair vibration and proprioception on the right below the lesion, because these fibres ascend ipsilaterally in the spinal cord.

Why can a brainstem lesion affect cranial nerves and long tracts together?

Cranial nerve nuclei or fascicles and ascending/descending long tracts lie close together in the brainstem. A single lesion can therefore combine an ipsilateral cranial nerve deficit with long-tract findings affecting the body.

What is the safest way to memorise a tract?

Draw its start, relay, crossing, ascent or descent, and endpoint; then explain which side is affected by a lesion above versus below its crossing.

Guide 03 · Function

Physiology: understand the mechanism, not just the definition

Physiology explains how cells and organs perform their functions and keep the internal environment within workable limits. Use the sequence: stimulus → sensor or receptor → control centre or signalling pathway → effector → response → feedback.

Homeostasis and feedback

Homeostasis is dynamic regulation, not a perfectly fixed internal state. A regulated variable is sensed, compared with a target range or control signal, and adjusted by effectors.

Negative feedback opposes a change and stabilises the variable; examples include temperature regulation and blood glucose control. Positive feedback amplifies a process until a defined endpoint; examples include the oxytocin-driven intensification of uterine contractions during labour and the coagulation cascade.

Cell membrane and transport

The lipid bilayer separates intracellular and extracellular fluid. Simple diffusion moves substances down their concentration or electrochemical gradient; facilitated diffusion uses channels or carriers without directly consuming ATP; primary active transport uses energy directly, while secondary active transport uses an ion gradient established by another transporter.

Osmosis is net water movement across a selectively permeable membrane in response to effective solute differences. Tonicity predicts the effect of a solution on cell volume and depends mainly on non-penetrating solutes.

Resting membrane potential and action potential

Unequal ion distributions and selective membrane permeability establish the resting membrane potential; the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase maintains gradients over time. In many neurons, threshold depolarisation opens voltage-gated sodium channels, producing a rapid upstroke, followed by sodium-channel inactivation and potassium conductance that repolarises the membrane.

Refractory periods limit immediate re-firing and help action potentials propagate in one direction. Myelin increases conduction speed through saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier.

Muscle contraction

In skeletal muscle, a motor neuron releases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction. Muscle action potentials trigger calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum; calcium binds troponin, shifts tropomyosin and permits actin–myosin cross-bridge cycling. ATP supports cross-bridge detachment and re-cocking, while calcium reuptake contributes to relaxation.

Cardiac muscle also uses calcium entry to trigger calcium release; smooth muscle uses calcium–calmodulin activation of myosin light-chain kinase. Do not assume all muscle types regulate contraction identically.

Cardiovascular physiology

Cardiac output equals heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Arterial pressure is influenced by cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance. Venous return, preload, contractility and afterload influence stroke volume. Arterioles are major resistance vessels; capillaries are specialised for exchange; veins provide capacitance and return blood to the heart.

Electrical activation precedes mechanical contraction. The SA node normally initiates the rhythm, AV nodal delay supports ventricular filling, and the His–Purkinje system rapidly distributes excitation through the ventricles.

Respiratory physiology

Ventilation moves air; external respiration exchanges gases between alveoli and pulmonary capillary blood; transport distributes gases; internal respiration exchanges gases between systemic blood and tissues. Oxygen diffuses down its partial-pressure gradient and is carried mainly bound to haemoglobin. Carbon dioxide travels dissolved, bound to proteins and largely as bicarbonate.

Alveolar ventilation, pulmonary perfusion, diffusion capacity and ventilation–perfusion matching all affect gas exchange. Carbon dioxide and hydrogen-ion changes are important drivers of ventilation, alongside oxygen sensing in appropriate conditions.

Renal physiology and fluid balance

Nephrons filter plasma at the glomerulus, selectively reabsorb useful substances, secrete selected substances into tubular fluid and excrete the remainder as urine. GFR reflects filtration forces and the filtration coefficient. The loop of Henle helps establish the medullary osmotic gradient; ADH changes collecting-duct water permeability; aldosterone promotes sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion in the distal nephron.

The kidneys also regulate acid–base balance, electrolytes, extracellular fluid volume, blood pressure and erythropoietin production.

Endocrine and gastrointestinal physiology

Hormones act through specific receptors and signalling pathways; peptide hormones commonly use cell-surface receptors, while steroid and thyroid hormones often alter gene transcription through intracellular receptors. Feedback regulates secretion.

Digestion involves motility, secretion, enzymatic breakdown, absorption and coordination by enteric nerves and hormones. The liver processes absorbed nutrients, produces bile and synthesises many plasma proteins; the pancreas contributes digestive enzymes and bicarbonate as well as endocrine control of glucose.

Physiology self-check

Why does negative feedback help maintain homeostasis?

It reduces deviation from the regulated range. For example, a rise in body temperature activates heat-loss responses that tend to lower temperature toward its regulated range.

How is facilitated diffusion different from active transport?

Facilitated diffusion moves down an electrochemical gradient through a protein without direct energy expenditure. Active transport moves a substance against its gradient using direct energy or energy stored in another gradient.

What happens to cardiac output if heart rate rises but stroke volume falls?

The net effect depends on the size of each change: cardiac output is heart rate × stroke volume. A higher rate does not guarantee a higher output if filling time and stroke volume fall enough.

Guide 04 · Microscopic structure

Histology: connect what you see under the microscope to what it does

Histology is not simply memorising tissue names. Identify the tissue by its architecture, cell arrangement, extracellular matrix, surface specialisations and relationship to the organ's function.

The four primary tissue classes

Epithelial tissue covers surfaces, lines cavities and forms glands; it has closely packed cells and a basement membrane. Classify it by layers and cell shape, then note specialisations such as cilia, microvilli or keratin.

Connective tissue supports and binds; its cells are distributed within extracellular matrix. Matrix composition distinguishes loose and dense connective tissues, cartilage, bone and blood.

Muscle tissue generates force: skeletal muscle is striated and usually voluntary, cardiac muscle is striated with intercalated discs, and smooth muscle lacks visible striations and regulates many hollow organs and vessels.

Nervous tissue contains neurons and supporting glia organised for signalling and integration.

A systematic slide-reading method

  1. Identify the organ and the overall low-power architecture.
  2. Find the lumen, capsule, layers, ducts, vessels or connective tissue boundaries.
  3. Move to higher power and classify the lining epithelium and key cell types.
  4. Look for diagnostic structures and their arrangement.
  5. State how the microscopic feature supports the organ's function.

Section orientation, staining, fixation and tissue processing can change appearances; interpret a feature in context rather than from one visual clue alone.

Guide 05 · Structural anatomy

Anatomy foundations and regional thinking

Use standard anatomical position as the reference frame, then describe each structure with consistent directional terms. When studying a region, work from superficial to deep and from boundaries to contents and relationships.

Describe location precisely

Use anatomical position, planes, axes, directions and regions consistently. Medial means nearer the median plane; lateral means farther from it. Proximal and distal are most useful along limbs or structures with a defined origin. Superficial and deep describe relative depth.

Describe a joint or organ in three dimensions: anterior/posterior, medial/lateral, superior/inferior and its relationships to nearby structures. Avoid ambiguous terms such as “above” when a standard anatomical direction is clearer.

Regional anatomy checklist

For every region, learn boundarieslayerscontentsblood supplyinnervationlymph drainagesurface landmarksclinical relevance.

For a muscle, add origin, insertion, action and nerve supply. For a vessel, trace origin, course, branches and territory. For a nerve, trace roots, course, branches, motor supply, sensory territory and common injury patterns.
