A guided academic roadmap

Start with foundations. Build towards mastery.

The resource centre is organised as a learning journey: learn the language of anatomy first, understand normal structure, then connect structure with function, development, imaging and clinical practice.

Recommended starting order1. History and scope2. Anatomical position and terminology3. Planes, axes and movements4. Regions, cavities, lines and surface landmarks5. Cells and tissues6. Systems, development and clinical application
01

General & Descriptive Anatomy

History, terminology, position, planes, directions, regions, cavities, divisions, lines, landmarks and anatomical variation.

02

Gross / Macroscopic Anatomy

Regional, systemic and surface anatomy; bones, joints, muscles, viscera, vessels, nerves and relationships between structures.

03

Microscopic Anatomy

Cytology and histology: cells, organelles, epithelium, connective tissue, cartilage, bone, muscle, nerves and organ microstructure.

04

Neuroanatomy

Brain, spinal cord, meninges, ventricles, cranial nerves, spinal nerves, tracts, autonomic pathways and functional localisation.

05

Embryology & Development

Gametes, fertilisation, implantation, germ layers, folding, organogenesis, placenta, fetal circulation and congenital anomalies.

06

Surface & Radiological Anatomy

Palpable landmarks, anatomical lines, projections, sectional anatomy and interpreting X-ray, CT, MRI and ultrasound orientation.

07

Human Physiology

Cell physiology, homeostasis, nerve and muscle, blood, cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, digestive, endocrine and reproductive function.

08

Clinical & Applied Anatomy

Use anatomical relationships to understand examination, injury patterns, imaging, common clinical signs and procedural principles.

This is an expanding curriculum, not a claim that every university syllabus is identical. Lesson depth and coverage will continue to grow in stages.

Revision that sticks

Memory aids and mnemonics.

Use each mnemonic as a retrieval cue, then explain the anatomy in your own words. A mnemonic supports learning; it does not replace understanding.

NEUROANATOMY

Cranial nerve function pattern

“Some Say Marry Money, But My Brother Says Big Brains Matter More.”

First-pass sensory/motor cue for I–XII. Check each nerve’s actual function.
NEURAL PATHWAYS

Where sensory pathways cross

DCML crosses in the medulla; pain and temperature cross in the spinal cord.

Pair modality, crossing level and lesion pattern.
EMBRYOLOGY

Remember the early weeks

1 Divide · 2 Two layers · 3 Three layers · 4 Fold and close · 5–8 Build organ plans.

Approximate post-fertilisation weeks; gestational age is usually about two weeks ahead.
GERM LAYERS

Three layers, broad patterns

Ectoderm: epidermis and nervous system. Mesoderm: muscle, bone, vessels. Endoderm: gut and respiratory epithelial linings.

Many organs combine derivatives from more than one layer.
MENINGES

Coverings of the CNS

Dura → Arachnoid → Pia (“DAP”).

CSF circulates in the subarachnoid space.
FETAL CIRCULATION

Three key shunts

D-F-D: Ductus venosus, Foramen ovale, Ductus arteriosus.

Remember what each shunt bypasses, not only its name.

Cardiovascular physiology

Heart conduction pathway

Trace the normal electrical impulse through the conducting system and connect it to coordinated atrial and ventricular contraction.

PhysiologyECG foundationsClinical correlation
Self-test: name the sequence

Answer: Sinoatrial (SA) node → atrial myocardium → atrioventricular (AV) node (physiological delay) → atrioventricular bundle (bundle of His) → right and left bundle branches → Purkinje fibres → ventricular myocardium.

Clinical link: Conduction blocks can alter the timing or route of ventricular activation and may change the ECG.

Renal anatomy & physiology

Nephron: the functional unit of the kidney

Follow filtrate from the renal corpuscle through the tubules, then relate each segment to filtration, reabsorption, secretion and urine concentration.

HistologyRenal physiologyFluid balance
Self-test: where does filtration begin?

Answer: Filtration occurs across the glomerular filtration barrier from glomerular capillaries into Bowman's (glomerular) space. The filtrate then enters the proximal convoluted tubule.

Clinical link: Damage to the filtration barrier may allow abnormal amounts of protein or blood cells to appear in urine; interpretation requires appropriate clinical testing.

Anatomical orientation

The three principal anatomical planes

Use the sagittal, frontal (coronal) and transverse (horizontal/axial) planes to describe sections, movements and the orientation of structures in anatomy and medical imaging.

FoundationsImagingSpatial orientation
Self-test: which plane separates anterior from posterior?

Answer: The frontal (coronal) plane separates anterior (front) from posterior (back) portions. The sagittal plane separates left and right portions; the transverse plane separates superior and inferior portions.

Clinical link: CT and MRI images are commonly viewed in axial/transverse, coronal and sagittal planes, so plane recognition is essential for describing a lesion's location.
